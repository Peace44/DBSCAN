# Building a State-of-the-Art Perception System for a Formula SAE Driverless Racecar

Developing a high-performance perception stack for a Formula SAE Italy Driverless racecar requires combining advanced LiDAR processing, real-time computing optimizations, reliable cone detection/classification, robust SLAM, and event-specific tuning. This guide breaks down the key components and best practices:

## LiDAR-Based Perception Techniques

Modern Formula Student driverless cars rely heavily on LiDAR to perceive track boundaries defined by cones. A 64-channel Ouster LiDAR (e.g. OS1-64) provides dense 3D point clouds, which must be processed efficiently to detect small traffic cones accurately. State-of-the-art LiDAR perception involves: 

- **Ground Plane Removal:** Remove ground points so only obstacles (cones) remain. This can be done by fitting one or multiple planes to the ground. For example, a **multi-plane modeling** approach can better handle uneven terrain than a single RANSAC plane. One robust method modeled the ground as several planes and filtered it out, improving downstream cone extraction ([Essays.se: LIDAR PERCEPTION](https://www.essays.se/about/LiDAR+perception/?startrecord=16#:~:text=Abstract%20%3A%20Building%20an%20autonomous,READ%20MORE)) ([Essays.se: LIDAR PERCEPTION](https://www.essays.se/about/LiDAR+perception/?startrecord=16#:~:text=ground%20removal%20strategy%20for%203D,READ%20MORE)).

- **Efficient Clustering:** Cluster the remaining 3D points to find individual cone candidates. Traditional Euclidean clustering in 3D can be slow, so modern approaches exploit the LiDAR’s scan pattern. For instance, treating the LiDAR data as a range image (organized by laser channel and azimuth) allows using image segmentation techniques for clustering, achieving **20× faster** performance than naive methods ([Essays.se: LIDAR PERCEPTION](https://www.essays.se/about/LiDAR+perception/?startrecord=16#:~:text=Abstract%20%3A%20Building%20an%20autonomous,READ%20MORE)) ([Essays.se: LIDAR PERCEPTION](https://www.essays.se/about/LiDAR+perception/?startrecord=16#:~:text=ground%20removal%20strategy%20for%203D,READ%20MORE)). This means the algorithm can run in real-time even with tens of thousands of points per frame.

- **Feature Extraction for Cones:** For each cluster identified, compute features to verify it’s a cone. Useful features include the cluster’s height (cones are ~30 cm tall ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=Formula%20Student%20AI%20is%20an,As%20of%20the))), width, number of points, and intensity distribution. Some teams implement a *“cone legality check”* – ensuring the cluster’s dimensions match a cone and that it isn’t too sparse or too large ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=Cone%20reconstruction%20Cone%20legality%20verification,Image%20patch%20extraction%20Cone%20color)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=Fig,To%20achieve%20better%20cone%20detection)). This filters out spurious clusters (e.g. patches of grass or multiple cones merged).

- **Intensity and Reflectivity Cues:** Leverage the LiDAR’s intensity (reflectance) data to aid detection. Traffic cones often have reflective tape or distinct reflectivity patterns. A cutting-edge approach even uses a neural network on the LiDAR’s multi-channel intensity pattern to **classify cone color** (e.g. a yellow cone yields a characteristic bright-dark-bright return pattern across vertical beams) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=2,the%20vision%20and%20LiDAR%20pipelines)). While intensity-based color estimation is complex, it can complement camera-based methods when lighting is poor.

- **Sensor Calibration:** Calibrate the LiDAR coordinate frame to the vehicle and camera frames. Accurate extrinsic calibration ensures that once a cone is detected in 3D, its position can be transformed correctly for mapping and sensor fusion. Ouster LiDARs typically provide time-synchronized packets, so also ensure timestamps are in sync (perhaps via a common clock) to fuse with other sensors.

By applying aggressive ground filtering and fast clustering, the LiDAR can reliably pick out cone locations in real time. Leading designs report robust 3D cone detection even as the car moves at high speeds ([Real-Time 3D Traffic Cone Detection for Autonomous Driving | NVIDIA Developer](https://developer.nvidia.com/embedded/community/jetson-projects/3d_traffic_cone_detection#:~:text=approach%2C%20,gotthard%20driverless)) ([Real-Time 3D Traffic Cone Detection for Autonomous Driving | NVIDIA Developer](https://developer.nvidia.com/embedded/community/jetson-projects/3d_traffic_cone_detection#:~:text=drive%20autonomously%20on%20an%20unseen,gotthard%20driverless)), which is critical for mapping the track.

## Real-Time Processing with NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin

The Jetson AGX Orin is a powerful edge computer (up to **275 TOPS of AI performance**) designed for real-time autonomous systems ([Foresight integrates NVIDIA Jetson Orin into its perception technologies - The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/foresight-integrates-nvidia-jetson-orin-perception-technologies/#:~:text=These%20platforms%20reduce%20power%20consumption,precise%20terrain%20mapping%20in%20complex)). To harness this power for perception on a driverless racecar, the software must be optimized for speed and efficiency:

- **Optimized Object Detection & Segmentation:** Use lightweight, efficient models and algorithms that fit the Orin’s GPU and DLAccelerators. For vision-based cone detection, models like **TinyYOLOv3/YOLOv5** can run at high FPS when optimized with TensorRT. For example, teams have deployed cone-detection CNNs on the older Jetson TX2 at 30 FPS ([Real-Time 3D Traffic Cone Detection for Autonomous Driving | NVIDIA Developer](https://developer.nvidia.com/embedded/community/jetson-projects/3d_traffic_cone_detection#:~:text=approach%2C%20,gotthard%20driverless)), so the AGX Orin can easily handle such networks at higher frame rates with proper optimization. Use **TensorRT** to convert trained models to low-precision (FP16/INT8) engines, greatly accelerating inference on the Orin. Similarly, any point cloud segmentation (if using deep learning) should leverage the GPU – though in practice classical clustering (as above) on the CPU may suffice due to Orin’s strong multi-core CPU.

- **Pipelining and Parallelism:** Structure the perception stack into parallel threads or processes to utilize the Orin’s multi-core architecture. For instance, LiDAR clustering can run on CPU in parallel with camera CNN on the GPU. Orin can handle **concurrent** workloads – you might dedicate certain CPU cores to the LiDAR pipeline and others to the SLAM filter, while the GPU handles neural network inference. This reduces latency by avoiding sequential processing of sensor data.

- **Memory and I/O Efficiency:** Minimize copy overhead between processes. Using ROS2 with **zero-copy transport** (type adaptation/negotiation) allows sharing image and point cloud data without expensive memory copies ([Improve Perception Performance for ROS 2 Applications with NVIDIA Isaac Transport for ROS | NVIDIA Technical Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improve-perception-performance-for-ros-2-applications-with-nvidia-isaac-transport-for-ros/#:~:text=Accelerating%20processing%20pipelines%20using%20type,gains%20enabled%20by%20the%20hardware)) ([Improve Perception Performance for ROS 2 Applications with NVIDIA Isaac Transport for ROS | NVIDIA Technical Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improve-perception-performance-for-ros-2-applications-with-nvidia-isaac-transport-for-ros/#:~:text=Type%20adaptation%20and%20negotiation%20have,impressive%207x%20improvement%20on%20Orin)). This is especially important when handling high-bandwidth LiDAR data at 10–20 Hz and high-resolution camera frames at 30 Hz. ROS2 Humble’s new features have shown up to *7× improvement* in throughput on Orin by eliminating unnecessary CPU load ([Improve Perception Performance for ROS 2 Applications with NVIDIA Isaac Transport for ROS | NVIDIA Technical Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improve-perception-performance-for-ros-2-applications-with-nvidia-isaac-transport-for-ros/#:~:text=Type%20adaptation%20and%20negotiation%20have,impressive%207x%20improvement%20on%20Orin)).

- **GPU Acceleration in PCL:** If using Point Cloud Library (PCL) for processing, consider GPU-accelerated versions for expensive steps. For example, there are implementations of Euclidean clustering and voxel grid filtering using CUDA. The Orin’s 2048 CUDA cores can significantly speed up point cloud operations that are parallelizable.

- **Real-Time Scheduling:** Because dynamic events require strict timing (e.g., detecting a cone and braking in time), configure the system for real-time performance. This may include using a real-time Linux kernel on the Orin and setting ROS2 executor to use real-time thread priorities. Ensuring deterministic timing for critical perception tasks prevents latency spikes that could cause missed detections at high speed.

- **Thermal and Power Considerations:** The Orin will be under heavy load; make sure to manage its thermal profile (adequate cooling) to avoid throttling, which can reduce processing performance. Optimize algorithms to run within the ~50W power budget so that the board maintains peak clock rates. Techniques like only running heavy neural nets when needed (e.g., skip some frames if not critical) or using the Orin’s power modes can help maintain real-time performance over a long event.

By carefully optimizing the software pipeline and leveraging Orin’s hardware accelerators, the perception system can achieve real-time cone detection and mapping. NVIDIA reported that Jetson AGX Orin enables *“real-time data processing, advanced obstacle detection, and precise mapping in complex environments”* ([Foresight integrates NVIDIA Jetson Orin into its perception technologies - The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/foresight-integrates-nvidia-jetson-orin-perception-technologies/#:~:text=These%20platforms%20reduce%20power%20consumption,precise%20terrain%20mapping%20in%20complex)) – exactly the capabilities needed for an autonomous racer. In practice, this means the car can detect and react to cones on-the-fly without lag, even as it navigates challenging courses.

## Cone Recognition and Classification

**Recognizing and classifying cones by color** (yellow, blue, orange) is crucial, since the racecar must distinguish left-boundary vs right-boundary cones and special markers. The state-of-the-art approach combines machine learning on camera images with data from LiDAR:

- **Vision-Based Cone Detection:** High-performing teams use neural networks on the camera feed to detect cones and identify their colors. A popular choice is the YOLO family of networks due to their speed and accuracy. For instance, one team trained a **Tiny-YOLOv3 CNN** to detect blue and yellow cones in real time ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=overall%20accuracy%20and%20robustness,LiDAR%20pipeline%20consists%20of%20three)). The network outputs bounding boxes and class (color) for each cone in the image. Modern YOLOv5/YOLOv8 models could also be used, potentially giving better accuracy; these can be pruned or quantized to run fast on the Jetson. The output of the vision system is a list of cone detections with color labels.

- **3D Position Estimation:** A single camera can provide color, but determining distance requires either stereovision or using LiDAR. One approach is to **project LiDAR clusters into the camera** and associate them with YOLO detections ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=detection%20Cone%20classification%20Fig,It%20comprises%20two)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=Fig,is%20imple%02mented%20by%20our%20early)). This gives the best of both: the LiDAR provides precise range to the cone, and the camera provides the color classification. Another approach (if LiDAR is not used for a particular cone) is to estimate distance from the monocular image using the known cone size. For example, by comparing the pixel height of the cone in the image to its actual 30 cm height, an approximate range can be calculated ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=overall%20accuracy%20and%20robustness,LiDAR%20pipeline%20consists%20of%20three)). This was used in earlier designs to project detected cones into 3D space when LiDAR data wasn’t fused. However, monocular distance estimates can be noisy, so sensor fusion is preferred for accuracy.

- **Deep Learning Classification:** In addition to object detection networks, some teams train a **classification model** that takes a cropped cone image and outputs the cone’s color (or type). This can be useful if using a two-stage vision pipeline (first find object, then classify color with a smaller CNN). Given the simplicity of distinguishing cone colors (essentially a color recognition task), even classical methods (checking the average hue in the bounding box in HSV color space) can work in controlled lighting. However, a learned model is more robust to varying lighting or partial occlusions (e.g., dusty cones). Ensure the model is trained on images of cones in various lighting conditions (sunlight, shade, different backgrounds) for robustness.

- **Training Data and Dataset Benchmarking:** Create or use a cone dataset that includes **blue, yellow, and orange cones** from Formula Student contexts. The cones are standardized (approx. 30 cm tall, often with reflective tape) ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=Formula%20Student%20AI%20is%20an,As%20of%20the)). Some public datasets (e.g., the *TraCon dataset* for traffic cones) or data from past competitions can be used as a starting point, but it’s best to gather your own images from testing sessions. Collect images from the vehicle’s onboard cameras while driving around cones, then label them (bounding box + class). Aim for a diverse dataset: different track surfaces (asphalt, grass), various times of day, and include the small and large orange cones used for start/finish or other markers. **Benchmark your cone detection model** on a held-out test set of images: you should achieve high precision and recall (e.g. >95% detection rate) so that almost no cones are missed or misclassified during a run. Misclassifying a cone’s color can confuse the map (e.g., swapping left/right), so emphasize accuracy in classification. If using a LiDAR-only classification method (intensity pattern), similarly validate it against ground truth colors – one study achieved reliable color estimates by recognizing intensity patterns unique to each cone color ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=2,the%20vision%20and%20LiDAR%20pipelines)), but this requires high-resolution LiDAR returns and careful calibration.

- **Combining Vision and LiDAR Outputs:** Fuse the camera and LiDAR information to get robust cone detection. A common strategy:
  - Use LiDAR clustering to get the 3D position of each cone-like object.
  - Project those 3D positions into the camera image (given known calibration) to find corresponding pixels/regions ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=detection%20Cone%20classification%20Fig,It%20comprises%20two)).
  - Use the color information from that image region (either from the CNN detection or a simple color read) to assign the cone a class (blue, yellow, orange). This **early sensor fusion** approach was shown to improve overall accuracy and robustness ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=performance%20an%20early%20fusion%20approach,Utilizing%20the%20cones%20as)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=an%20Unscented%20Kalman%20Filter%20,of%20local%20and%20global%20mapping)).
  - If the camera fails to detect a cone that LiDAR sees (e.g., in low-light), you may fall back to LiDAR intensity-based classification or mark it as unknown – having redundancy improves reliability.

- **Real-Time Considerations:** Ensure that the cone recognition pipeline runs within the sensor frame rates. Using the Jetson Orin, a model like TinyYOLOv3 can run in a few milliseconds ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=overall%20accuracy%20and%20robustness,LiDAR%20pipeline%20consists%20of%20three)), so it should keep up with a 30 FPS camera. If needed, reduce image resolution or crop to the region of interest (e.g., focus on the lower half of the image where cones appear) to speed up processing. Also, since LiDAR data is slower (10–20 Hz), you might update cone positions at that rate but update color classification at the camera rate; be prepared to handle slight mismatches (e.g., a cone moved in the interim – usually negligible given small cone motion relative to car).

**Benchmarking your model** on real track data and refining it is crucial. Use metrics like **precision, recall, and F1-score** for each cone color. Additionally, measure the *distance error* of the perceived cone positions (especially if using vision for range): this can be done by comparing against a LiDAR truth or known track layout. A high-performing system will detect all cones correctly and place them within ~0.1–0.2 m of their actual position ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=creating%20accurate%20maps,in%20popularity%20in%20the%20past)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=show%20improved%20accuracy%20when%20driving,29%20m)), enabling accurate mapping.

## SLAM, Object Detection, and Obstacle Avoidance

Once cones are detected and classified, the next challenge is to **localize the vehicle and map the track** (SLAM) while avoiding any obstacles (primarily the cones themselves). In Formula SAE Driverless, the cones act as landmarks for SLAM algorithms:

- **Landmark-Based SLAM:** Represent each cone as a landmark in a SLAM framework. The vehicle’s goal is to simultaneously build a map of cone positions and estimate its own pose. A common solution is an **Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) SLAM** or **particle-filter SLAM** tailored to sparse landmarks. For example, one team’s system fused observations from camera and LiDAR into a SLAM algorithm that maintained a coherent map of cones and the car’s pose within that map ([The track layout of a trackdrive discipline (FSG, 2018). Blue and... | Download Scientific Diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-track-layout-of-a-trackdrive-discipline-FSG-2018-Blue-and-yellow-cones-mark-the_fig1_343447423#:~:text=,)). An EKF can be used with state vector including the car pose and possibly the positions of cones (or the map can be handled separately). Each time a cone is observed, the filter updates the belief of the car’s position and the cone’s position. Fast data association (matching new observations to known landmarks) is crucial – algorithms like nearest-neighbor or Mahalanobis gating can match detected cones to the map, or more advanced methods like a joint compatibility branch and bound (JCBB) if multiple cones are observed at once.

- **High-Rate Localization Fusion:** In addition to landmark updates, use the IMU and wheel odometry for **motion updates** at a high rate. The car’s pose can be propagated at, say, 200 Hz using IMU gyros/accel and wheel speeds ([The track layout of a trackdrive discipline (FSG, 2018). Blue and... | Download Scientific Diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-track-layout-of-a-trackdrive-discipline-FSG-2018-Blue-and-yellow-cones-mark-the_fig1_343447423#:~:text=,)). This provides a continuous estimate of where the car is, even between LiDAR frames. One approach is dividing SLAM into two parts: a **localizer** that integrates velocities (IMU/GNSS/encoder) to update pose very fast, and a **mapper** that processes cone observations to correct the pose and build the map ([The track layout of a trackdrive discipline (FSG, 2018). Blue and... | Download Scientific Diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-track-layout-of-a-trackdrive-discipline-FSG-2018-Blue-and-yellow-cones-mark-the_fig1_343447423#:~:text=,)) ([The track layout of a trackdrive discipline (FSG, 2018). Blue and... | Download Scientific Diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-track-layout-of-a-trackdrive-discipline-FSG-2018-Blue-and-yellow-cones-mark-the_fig1_343447423#:~:text=,)). This two-tier architecture allows both high-frequency stability and lower-frequency global corrections.

- **Graph SLAM for Map Refinement:** As the vehicle completes a lap or revisits areas, performing a graph SLAM optimization can greatly improve accuracy. Teams often log the detected cone positions and poses, then run a backend optimization (e.g., least-squares minimization) to adjust the map. For instance, after an initial exploration lap, a **globally consistent map** of all cone positions can be computed with graph-based SLAM, reducing drift ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=an%20Unscented%20Kalman%20Filter%20,of%20local%20and%20global%20mapping)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=The%20local%20map%20is%20used,C)). This refined map is very useful for the Trackdrive (multiple lap) event. Graph SLAM can incorporate loop closures when the car recognizes previously seen cones from earlier in the lap, thereby **eliminating accumulated drift**.

- **Sensor Fusion for Accuracy:** Utilize **multi-sensor fusion** to improve localization. Integrating **GPS** (if available) can provide global position estimates, though many FS competitions run without GPS for the driverless category. More commonly, a high-grade **INS (IMU + GNSS)** or just IMU is used. For example, one design combined GPS-INS data with LiDAR odometry to achieve precise, high-rate localization ([[1809.07636] Autonomous Driving System Design for Formula Student Driverless Racecar](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/1809.07636#:~:text=,This%20paper%20also%20briefly)) ([[1809.07636] Autonomous Driving System Design for Formula Student Driverless Racecar](https://ar5iv.org/pdf/1809.07636#:~:text=autonomous%20racecar.%20A%20LIDAR,Finally%2C%20the%20system%20and)), but even without GPS, IMU aiding is vital. Use an Unscented Kalman Filter or EKF to fuse IMU, wheel speed, and possibly visual odometry (from camera or LiDAR scan matching) to keep the car’s pose estimate stable between cone observations ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=rotational%20velocities%20v%CB%86%20t%20%3D,to%20estimate%20the%20track%20boundaries)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=for%20ego,the%20Autocross%20discipline%20with%20Delaunay)). This ensures that when the LiDAR sees a cone, the system has a good guess of where it is, making data association easier and more accurate.

- **Object Detection and Obstacle Tracking:** Aside from mapping static cones, the perception system should watch for any **unexpected obstacles** on the track. While in FSD competitions the only intended obstacles are the cones, there is always a chance of a downed cone or debris. The LiDAR-based clustering will naturally pick up any object of similar size. If an object that is not a standard cone (e.g., a cone lying on its side or a spectator on track) is detected, the system should flag it. You might implement a simple rule: any cluster that doesn’t fit the cone shape or appears in the middle of the track is treated as a hazard. This could trigger a fallback behavior (like slowing down or maneuvering around it if possible). In essence, *obstacle detection* is built-in since avoiding cones = avoiding obstacles, but ensure your software doesn’t assume every detection is a valid cone – be ready to handle anomalies.

- **Adaptive Obstacle Avoidance:** At high speeds, the system must plan paths that avoid hitting cones with some margin. This is handled in the **path planning** stage, but perception provides the inputs. A good strategy is to inflate each cone’s perceived size in a costmap, so that the planner treats it as a larger obstacle and keeps a safe distance. If the vehicle is on a collision course with a cone due to uncertainty or late detection, the system can perform an emergency evade or stop. Real-time cone tracking (predicting where a cone is relative to car trajectory) is useful – however, since cones are static, this mostly comes down to precise localization of both car and cone. The **Ouster lidar-based localization** used in one project significantly improved their ability to clear gates without touching cones ([Enhanced localization for autonomous racing with high-resolution lidar | Ouster](https://ouster.com/insights/blog/enhanced-localization-for-autonomous-racing-with-high-resolution-lidar#:~:text=To%20complete%20the%20races%20in,lidar%20combined%20with%203D%20mapping)) ([Enhanced localization for autonomous racing with high-resolution lidar | Ouster](https://ouster.com/insights/blog/enhanced-localization-for-autonomous-racing-with-high-resolution-lidar#:~:text=choose%20the%20correct%20angles%20to,lidar%20combined%20with%203D%20mapping)). Essentially, centimeter-level localization accuracy helps ensure the car’s planned path stays exactly centered between cones. 

- **Fail-safes:** If any sensor goes out or a cone classification is uncertain, the system should fail safe. For example, if the camera is momentarily blinded by sun and cannot classify colors, the car could assume newly seen cones are of unknown color and still incorporate them in the map (perhaps using LiDAR intensity to guess). Redundancy between LiDAR and camera is advantageous – the system should be able to drive (perhaps slower) using LiDAR-only detection if the camera fails, and vice versa ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=overall%20accuracy%20and%20robustness,LiDAR%20pipeline%20consists%20of%20three)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=of%20a%20cone%20using%20only,data%20level%20exploits%20the%20respective)). This redundancy adds robustness for obstacle avoidance; even if one sensor misses a cone, the other can catch it so the vehicle doesn’t strike it.

In summary, use SLAM to build an accurate cone map and localize the car, and incorporate sensor fusion for reliability. With a solid map of cones, obstacle avoidance becomes a matter of planning a path that steers the car through the **gates** (space between left and right cones) without getting too close to any cone. The perception system’s job is to feed the planner with the latest cone positions and to flag anything unusual. A well-tuned SLAM (many teams use EKF or graph-based SLAM with cones ([The track layout of a trackdrive discipline (FSG, 2018). ## Optimization Strategies for Dynamic Events

Formula SAE Driverless competitions include several dynamic events – Acceleration, Skidpad, Autocross, and Trackdrive – each with different demands. The perception system can be configured or tuned for each to maximize performance:

### Acceleration

**Description:** A straight-line acceleration run (e.g. ~75 m straight track) where the car must launch from a standstill, go full throttle, and then come to a precise stop within a designated zone. Cones typically mark the lane edges and the finish line.

**Perception Challenges:** In acceleration, the primary task of perception is to **keep the car straight and know where to stop**. The cone layout is simple (straight line of cones on either side forming a lane). Key considerations:

- **Initial Alignment:** Ensure the car is aligned with the track centerline at the start. Using the cones at the starting gate (often two cones, one on each side of the start) to calibrate orientation can help. A quick LiDAR scan or camera look at the first few cones downrange can initialize the heading for a straight path.

- **High-Speed Updates:** As the car rapidly accelerates (0–100 kph in a few seconds), perception needs to verify it remains centered. A simple approach is to monitor the distance to left and right boundary cones using LiDAR – if one side’s cones start getting closer than the other, issue a slight steering correction. The processing must be real-time (but since the scenario is simple, even a 10 Hz LiDAR update is sufficient for minor corrections).

- **Detecting the Finish:** At the end, typically a set of **orange cones** or a gate indicates where to stop. The perception system should reliably detect these as early as possible. LiDAR can detect the finish cones at long range (30–40 m out) and measure distance to them. Complement this with an odometry-based distance estimate as a redundancy: the system knows the track length (per rules) and can cross-check that with what vision/LiDAR sees. As the car approaches the finish line, a precise distance measurement triggers the braking. For extra safety, some teams place a planar LiDAR or use the front camera to detect a physical banner or line if present, but usually cones are the indicator.

- **Filtering Noise:** At high acceleration, vibrations might introduce noise in sensors (especially cameras). Using the IMU to stabilize or filter measurements is helpful. Also, since only a few cones are in view at any time during acceleration, the perception algorithms can be simplified (e.g., filter out any object not aligned in a straight line ahead).

**Optimization:** Because the track is known to be straight, the perception stack can be simplified for speed – e.g., turn off SLAM loop closure and just do straightforward localization. Essentially, for Acceleration, once the car is pointed correctly, minimal perception input is needed aside from watching for the finish cones. Many teams focus on control (traction control, launch control) here, but ensuring the car doesn’t drift into a cone is still a perception task. In practice, a well-tuned system will keep the car arrow-straight down the lane and start braking exactly at the end cones (using LiDAR range or a preset distance). This yields a clean run with a precise stop, scoring maximum points.

### Skidpad

**Description:** A low-speed figure-8 (two circle) track where the car must complete two laps on each circle as quickly as possible without knocking cones. The layout is fixed: typically four cones mark the corners of two overlapping circles (one circle delineated by yellow cones, the other by blue cones, with an entry/exit gate).

**Perception Challenges:** Skidpad is about consistent, precision driving rather than high speed. The perception system should quickly recognize the **circular path** and help the car stay on that path:

- **Recognizing the Layout:** The system can exploit prior knowledge – the skidpad layout is predetermined (with exact cone positions known relative to the entry gate). Some teams hard-code the geometry and simply localize within it. For a vision-only approach, the car might circle slowly once to identify the cone arrangement. But since the rules allow known layout, the fastest approach is to treat the skidpad as a **predetermined map**: the car knows there are two circles of cones. Using the starting gate as reference, you can establish the coordinate frame, then you know where the other cones should be. Indeed, one implementation notes that a predetermined path is followed since exact cone locations are given for skidpad ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=4,Skidpad%20Event)) ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=A%20predetermined%20track%20is%20followed,red%2C%20green%20%26%20magenta)).

- **Tracking Cones Through Turns:** As the car circles, the perception system will continuously see cones on the inside and outside of the current circle. LiDAR is very useful here because it provides 360° coverage; even when the camera’s view is partly obstructed (e.g., when the car is perpendicular to a cone), the LiDAR still sees the distances. The system should maintain awareness of all four corner cones and the center “crossover” cones (often small orange markers at the center of the figure-8). A simple state machine can be used: first identify the first circle’s cones and drive around them, then switch to looking for the second circle’s cones.

- **Path Following Precision:** The skidpad requires maintaining a consistent radius (close to the cones but not hitting them). The perception output (cone positions) can be used to fit a circle (or two half-circles) that the car should follow. For example, after detecting the four corner cones, fit two circle arcs through them to determine the optimal inner and outer boundaries, then compute a path offset slightly from the inner cones to account for the car’s width and desired clearance. The car will then essentially do a **pure pursuit** or similar path following along that inferred circle ([The track layout of a trackdrive discipline (FSG, 2018). Blue and... | Download Scientific Diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-track-layout-of-a-trackdrive-discipline-FSG-2018-Blue-and-yellow-cones-mark-the_fig1_343447423#:~:text=Context%208)) ([The track layout of a trackdrive discipline (FSG, 2018). Blue and... | Download Scientific Diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-track-layout-of-a-trackdrive-discipline-FSG-2018-Blue-and-yellow-cones-mark-the_fig1_343447423#:~:text=,without%20the%20GSS%20compared)).

- **Low-Speed Optimizations:** Since skidpad is low speed (~8–10 m/s max, often less), perception can run at a relaxed pace. The car will likely rely on a high-fidelity map of cone positions rather than needing split-second updates. One can prioritize **accuracy over speed** here – e.g., averaging multiple LiDAR scans to get very precise cone positions before starting the maneuver. Also, fewer sensors could be used (some teams even do skidpad purely with cameras if reliable). However, caution: the tight turning can introduce IMU drift or wheel slip affecting localization. Using the cones as landmarks each lap corrects any drift (since you see the same cones every loop, the SLAM can correct the pose).

**Optimization:** Leverage the known skidpad geometry. Essentially, perception for skidpad might be as simple as detecting the first few cones to confirm positioning, then following a known circle path. Many teams effectively treat this event as a **path-following problem** with minimal SLAM – the vehicle’s path is divided into segments (entry, circle 1, transition, circle 2, exit) which are known in advance ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=4,Skidpad%20Event)) ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=A%20predetermined%20track%20is%20followed,red%2C%20green%20%26%20magenta)). The perception system just needs to ensure no unexpected deviation (like if a cone was misplaced). By simplifying perception, the car can focus on maintaining optimal tire slip (which is handled by control). This yields a smooth trajectory with minimal understeer or oversteer, crucial for a fast skidpad time.

### Autocross (Unknown Track, Single Lap)

**Description:** A single lap on an **unknown track** defined by cones (often a twisty course with slaloms, hairpins, etc.). The car has not seen the track before, so it must discover the cone layout in real time and complete the lap quickly. In some competitions, Autocross is a single attempt on a new track.

**Perception Challenges:** Autocross is the ultimate test of the perception system’s ability to map on the fly. The system must **build a map quickly and plan the route in real-time**:

- **Rapid SLAM Initialization:** At the start, the car has no prior map. The perception system should initialize SLAM as it moves, using the first cones it sees. A common strategy is to drive at a moderate speed initially (e.g. ~3 m/s) while beginning to map ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=creating%20accurate%20maps,in%20popularity%20in%20the%20past)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=show%20improved%20accuracy%20when%20driving,29%20m)). As confidence in the map grows, speed can increase. The key is that from the first few cone observations, the system begins to form the corridor (left/right boundaries).

- **Continuous Mapping:** The LiDAR and camera will be detecting new cones every second. These need to be integrated into the map without causing big latency. An **incremental mapping** approach (as opposed to batch) is required. EKF-SLAM or particle SLAM can update the map every time a new cone is observed. If the car detects cones that form a new segment of track, the planner should immediately use that information to adjust the path. Modern systems use a layered mapping: a **local map** that fuses data in real-time for immediate planning, and a **global map** that refines things in the background ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=an%20Inertial%20Measurement%20Unit%2C%20dual,GraphSLAM%20to%20represent%20the%20cone)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=an%20Unscented%20Kalman%20Filter%20,of%20local%20and%20global%20mapping)).

- **Planning in Unknown Environments:** Because not all cones are known ahead, the planner must handle partial information. One approach is a **“follow the middle”** algorithm – always aim for the center between the nearest left and right cone ahead. As new cones appear, adjust the path smoothly toward the new centerline. Another approach uses **Delaunay triangulation** on the known cone map to infer the likely track edges and center ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=an%20Unscented%20Kalman%20Filter%20,of%20local%20and%20global%20mapping)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=cone%20observations%20from%20the%20perception,A%20globally%20consistent)). Essentially, the algorithm can guess how cones pair up as left/right and then draw a mid-line. Bayesian filters can also be used to predict where the next cones might be (e.g., if you see a series of alternating left-right cones, you expect that pattern to continue) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=an%20Unscented%20Kalman%20Filter%20,of%20local%20and%20global%20mapping)).

- **Adaptive Speed Control:** Perception should inform the planning module of the track geometry ahead so that speed can be adjusted. For example, if a tight turn is detected late (cones suddenly indicate a sharp bend), the system may need to decelerate rapidly to make the turn. Conversely, if a straight segment is identified, it can accelerate. This requires perceiving as far ahead as possible: the 64-channel LiDAR can see cones up to 30–40 m out; use that to anticipate upcoming curves. The challenge is if the course has a sudden hairpin, the car might not see the exit cones until it’s quite close. A strategy here is to err on the side of caution: approach unknown areas a bit slower until cones reveal the path. The advanced 2019 AMZ system used a Bayesian planning approach to safely raise unknown-track speed from 3 m/s to 12 m/s while keeping mapping error low (0.29 m) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=approach%2C%20and%20a%20planning%20approach,29%20m)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=creating%20accurate%20maps,in%20popularity%20in%20the%20past)), showing that with better perception and filtering, one can push faster even without prior knowledge.

- **Stability and Recovery:** The perception system should handle imperfect data gracefully. If one cone is missed or a false positive is introduced, the SLAM should not freak out. Applying smoothing or filtering to the estimated track can avoid sudden zig-zags. If at any point the system is unsure (e.g., it loses track of whether a cone is left or right side), a safe approach is to slow down and proceed until it resolves the ambiguity (possibly using the next cone to disambiguate).

**Optimization:** The Autocross event is won by the car that can best **explore while racing**. Focus on a perception stack that is robust to new input and quickly converges to a reliable track map. Testing this in a variety of unknown layouts (you can set up random cone patterns and practice) will train the system to handle surprises. Logging data from these runs and analyzing failures (e.g., did the system mis-classify a cone and thus think the track went the wrong way?) helps improve the algorithms. The end goal: the car should complete the lap on the first try, at a brisk pace, with no cones hit. A state-of-the-art system in 2018–2019 was able to autonomously map and drive an unseen track, achieving 54 km/h top speed, which contributed to winning competitions ([Real-Time 3D Traffic Cone Detection for Autonomous Driving | NVIDIA Developer](https://developer.nvidia.com/embedded/community/jetson-projects/3d_traffic_cone_detection#:~:text=approach%2C%20,gotthard%20driverless)) ([Real-Time 3D Traffic Cone Detection for Autonomous Driving | NVIDIA Developer](https://developer.nvidia.com/embedded/community/jetson-projects/3d_traffic_cone_detection#:~:text=drive%20autonomously%20on%20an%20unseen,gotthard%20driverless)). This was possible thanks to robust perception enabling confident driving. Your implementation should strive for that level of reliability, using all available sensor data to avoid mistakes.

### Trackdrive (Known Track, Multiple Laps)

**Description:** Trackdrive is a multi-lap race (e.g., 10 laps) on a track that the car has already seen (usually the same track as Autocross or after a mapping run). The goal is to achieve the fastest lap times once the track is known, without driver input of course.

**Perception Challenges:** With the map known, the emphasis shifts to **precision and consistency**. The perception system’s role is to localize the car on the known map and detect any changes (like a moved cone), enabling the car to push to the limit:

- **Using the Known Map:** After Autocross, you will have a detailed map of all cone positions (perhaps refined with GraphSLAM for accuracy). In Trackdrive, load this map into the system. The perception task becomes **localization**: matching observed cones to the map to continually refine the car’s position on track. Techniques like **Monte Carlo Localization (particle filter)** using the cone map or an EKF using known landmarks can give very precise pose estimates. For example, Ouster LiDAR mapping was used to create an HD 3D map, and then a lighter LiDAR was used each lap to localize within that map, yielding centimeter-level accuracy and winning results ([Enhanced localization for autonomous racing with high-resolution lidar | Ouster](https://ouster.com/insights/blog/enhanced-localization-for-autonomous-racing-with-high-resolution-lidar#:~:text=start%20from%20scratch%20for%20the,optimized%20them%20for%20racing%20conditions)) ([Enhanced localization for autonomous racing with high-resolution lidar | Ouster](https://ouster.com/insights/blog/enhanced-localization-for-autonomous-racing-with-high-resolution-lidar#:~:text=The%20ARG%20team%20first%20mapped,resolution%203D%20map)).

- **High-Precision Localization:** With a map, the car can utilize **point cloud matching** (ICP or NDT algorithms) to localize, in addition to landmark-based methods. Some teams integrate Autoware.AI modules for scan matching on LiDAR ([Enhanced localization for autonomous racing with high-resolution lidar | Ouster](https://ouster.com/insights/blog/enhanced-localization-for-autonomous-racing-with-high-resolution-lidar#:~:text=To%20improve%20the%20robustness%2C%20accuracy%2C,optimized%20them%20for%20racing%20conditions)). Essentially, the current LiDAR scan is matched to the prior map to get a pose correction. This can achieve very low drift. The ARG team did this with an OS1-64 and reported improved localization over GPS, contributing to the fastest lap times ([Enhanced localization for autonomous racing with high-resolution lidar | Ouster](https://ouster.com/insights/blog/enhanced-localization-for-autonomous-racing-with-high-resolution-lidar#:~:text=Driving%20the%20field%20forward)) ([Enhanced localization for autonomous racing with high-resolution lidar | Ouster](https://ouster.com/insights/blog/enhanced-localization-for-autonomous-racing-with-high-resolution-lidar#:~:text=High%20resolution%20digital%20lidar%20unlocks,second%20place%20at%20Zala%20Zone)).

- **Change Detection:** The system should verify that cones are where they were before. If a cone is knocked down or moved between runs, the perception needs to catch that and update the map or at least avoid that cone. This can be done by comparing the expected cone location (from map) to what sensors see. If a previously mapped cone is not detected for several frames, assume it fell or got moved. In such cases, it might effectively widen the track at that spot (the car could drive through where the cone was, which is allowed if the cone is down, but teams often still avoid the base). Conversely, if a new cone appears (maybe a marshal accidentally left something), treat it as a new obstacle. These situations are rare but handling them ensures robustness.

- **Optimal Racing Line:** With known cone positions, the perception system can assist in computing the **optimal racing line**. This is more of a planning task, but perception provides the map and can even provide friction estimates (if the car has tire sensors or if it can infer surface conditions). The goal is to minimize lap time by choosing a path that maximizes average speed. This often means a wider line through corners (using the full track width). The map can be input into a path optimization algorithm – for example, form a spline through the track centerline and then optimize offsets at each turn. There are approaches using optimal control or simple heuristics to find apexes. Regardless, once the target path is set, perception’s job is to make sure the car is **tracking it accurately** each lap.

- **Consistency Over Laps:** During the 10 laps, the perception system should maintain performance despite potential changes like lighting (if outdoors, lighting can change lap to lap), dust on sensors, etc. Ensure the camera exposure or LiDAR calibration remains consistent. Monitor for any drift in the SLAM estimate and correct via periodic map alignment. Since the laps are repetitive, you can even incorporate a lap-by-lap averaging: e.g., after each lap, compare the recorded trajectory to the known map to see if there’s any systematic localization bias, then correct for the next lap.

**Optimization:** Trackdrive allows pushing the vehicle to its limits because the perception is no longer guessing the track – it knows it. Thus, you can tune the perception filters to be less cautious: for example, increase the confidence in the map so that the car isn’t slowing for unknown turns (since there are none unknown). The focus is on **accuracy** – a small error in perceiving a cone could lead to clipping it at high speed. Using high-resolution sensing and fusing everything (camera + LiDAR + IMU + even GNSS if available) is recommended for maximal accuracy. The outcome should be a series of very fast laps. Often the fastest lap is towards the end once the car and perception have fully calibrated to the track. Some teams also gradually raise a speed limiter each lap (e.g., 80% of max on first lap, then 90%, then 100%) to safely find the limit. Perception data can inform this – if the car had a near-miss with a cone on a lap, maybe don’t increase speed further; if it had a comfortable clearance, you can push more.

In essence, by the time you reach Trackdrive, the perception system is running at full capability – mapping is solved, and it’s all about precision localization and monitoring. A well-implemented system will enable the car to repeatedly zip through the course on rails, with minimal cone clearance and maximum speed, resulting in competitive lap times across all 10 runs.

## Implementation Guide

Finally, we outline how to implement this perception stack in practice, including recommended software frameworks and a step-by-step integration plan, along with validation methodologies. 

### Frameworks, Libraries, and Tools

Building an autonomous racing perception system is greatly aided by existing frameworks and libraries:

- **ROS 2:** The Robot Operating System 2 is the backbone for many driverless car teams. ROS2 (e.g., Foxy or Humble) provides the middleware for sensor drivers, message passing, and modular development. It supports real-time communication and can exploit Zero-Copy features on the Jetson for efficiency ([Improve Perception Performance for ROS 2 Applications with NVIDIA Isaac Transport for ROS | NVIDIA Technical Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improve-perception-performance-for-ros-2-applications-with-nvidia-isaac-transport-for-ros/#:~:text=Accelerating%20processing%20pipelines%20using%20type,gains%20enabled%20by%20the%20hardware)). Use ROS2 to integrate LiDAR (often via the Ouster ROS driver), cameras, and IMU, and to create separate nodes for detection, SLAM, planning, etc.

- **OpenCV:** A useful library for image processing. Even if using deep learning for cone detection, OpenCV can handle tasks like image undistortion (if using fisheye lenses), color space conversion (for simple HSV thresholding of cone colors), and visualization/debugging of detection results.

- **PCL (Point Cloud Library):** Essential for 3D LiDAR data processing. PCL offers functions for filtering (passthrough, voxel grid downsampling), ground plane segmentation (SAC models), and Euclidean clustering. Many of these algorithms can serve as a starting point before custom optimizations. PCL is well-tested and can handle large point clouds from a 64-channel LiDAR.

- **Deep Learning Frameworks:** *TensorFlow* or *PyTorch* for training your cone detection models. For runtime on Jetson, export to ONNX and use **TensorRT** for optimized inference. NVIDIA’s TensorRT will take your model and perform device-specific optimizations (layer fusion, precision calibration) to run efficiently on the Orin.

- **Pre-trained Models:** Models like YOLOv5 or YOLOv7 (which are state-of-the-art for object detection) can be fine-tuned on cone data. Likewise, segmentation models (DeepLab, etc.) could be trained to segment cone pixels if you choose a segmentation approach. There are also lighter models (MobileNet-SSD) if resources are constrained, though Orin can handle larger models easily. Also consider using **existing projects**: for example, the AMZ Driverless team has open-sourced parts of their 2018/2019 solutions including perception ([Real-Time 3D Traffic Cone Detection for Autonomous Driving | NVIDIA Developer](https://developer.nvidia.com/embedded/community/jetson-projects/3d_traffic_cone_detection#:~:text=approach%2C%20,gotthard%20driverless)) ([Real-Time 3D Traffic Cone Detection for Autonomous Driving | NVIDIA Developer](https://developer.nvidia.com/embedded/community/jetson-projects/3d_traffic_cone_detection#:~:text=drive%20autonomously%20on%20an%20unseen,gotthard%20driverless)).

- **SLAM Libraries:** While many teams implement custom SLAM, you could use frameworks like **GTSAM** (Georgia Tech Smoothing and Mapping library) for graph optimization, or **MRPT** (Mobile Robot Programming Toolkit) which has an EKF SLAM for landmarks ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=3,2%20Control%20algorithms)). These can accelerate development of the mapping/localization aspect. Ensure whatever you use can run in real-time on the Jetson.

- **Sensor Fusion and Filtering:** Libraries like **Robot Localization** (ROS package) provide ready-made EKF/UKF for fusing IMU, odometry, and GPS. You can configure these to output a fused pose at high rates. Additionally, if using a particle filter for localization, frameworks like **Adaptive Monte Carlo Localization (AMCL)** from ROS could be adapted to work with cones instead of 2D grid maps.

- **Visualization and Simulation:** Tools such as **RViz** (ROS visualization) are invaluable for debugging – you can visualize point clouds, detected cone markers, and the map during development. For simulation, consider using **Gazebo** or **Formula Student Driverless Simulator (FSDS)** to test algorithms on virtual tracks (some teams used FSDS to validate skidpad logic ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=white%20triggers,FSDS))). Simulation helps in safe testing of high-speed scenarios and corner cases.

### Step-by-Step Integration

Implementing the perception stack involves several stages. Below is a step-by-step guide:

1. **Sensor Setup and Calibration:**
   - Install and configure the Ouster 64-channel LiDAR. Use the manufacturer’s ROS2 driver to get point clouds publishing at the desired frequency. Calibrate the LiDAR’s mounting angle and position on the car so the coordinate frame is correct (e.g., the LiDAR frame relative to the base_link of the vehicle).
   - Install and configure the camera (or cameras). Ensure they are synchronized if using multiple (ZED stereo cameras, for instance, come with their SDK). Calibrate intrinsic parameters (camera lens) and extrinsics between camera and LiDAR. For extrinsic calibration, you can use a calibration target or manually adjust using a few observed cones to align the point cloud projections ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=detection%20Cone%20classification%20Fig,It%20comprises%20two)).
   - Connect the IMU and wheel encoder data. Use ROS2 drivers or CAN bus readers to get IMU messages (orientation, angular velocity, acceleration) and wheel speeds. Calibrate the IMU orientation and align its frame with the car’s frame.

2. **Pre-Processing (Filtering):**
   - Create a LiDAR processing node that subscribes to the raw point cloud. Implement ground removal (e.g., plane fitting or Markov Random Field approach for uneven ground). Verify by visualizing in RViz that ground points are largely gone and cones remain as clusters above ground.
   - Implement a voxel grid filter to downsample the point cloud if necessary (the Ouster has a lot of points; downsampling can reduce computation with minimal loss in 10–20m range detection).
   - (Optional) Do a **field-of-view crop**: e.g., ignore points that are very high or very low, or behind the vehicle, since cones will be near ground and generally in front/side of the car ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=trimming%20Projection%20into%20world%20frame,clustering%20Cone%20reconstruction%20Cone%20legality)).

3. **Cone Detection (LiDAR):**
   - Using the filtered cloud, perform clustering to identify cone candidates. Start with PCL’s Euclidean clustering with a distance threshold (maybe ~0.2m) tuned to separate individual cones. Limit cluster size (min points ~5, max points ~100 perhaps) to filter out noise or large objects.
   - For each cluster, compute its centroid and bounding box. Filter by expected cone dimensions (height ~0.3 m, roughly cylindrical shape). This yields a list of detected cones in 3D.
   - (Optional) Assign an ID to each cone via tracking: use a simple nearest neighbor in successive frames to give persistent IDs. This can help smooth the positions over time or fuse multiple observations.
   - Output: Publish the cone positions (perhaps as a custom message or as ROS2 markers for debugging). At this stage, they might be unlabeled (color unknown).

4. **Cone Classification (Vision):**
   - Set up the camera cone detection node. If using a CNN (YOLO), integrate it via TensorRT. Feed the camera images and get detected bounding boxes and classes. Filter these detections by confidence score to reduce false positives.
   - For each detected cone in the image, project a ray into 3D (if using monocular) to estimate where it could be, or if using stereo, triangulate for range. However, the better approach is to fuse with LiDAR:
   - Take the 3D cones from the LiDAR node and project them into the camera image (using calibration parameters). Then, for each projected 3D cone, check the color in the image:
       * If you have YOLO detections, see if the LiDAR-projected point falls inside a YOLO bounding box. If so, assign that color label to the LiDAR cone ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=detection%20Cone%20classification%20Fig,It%20comprises%20two)) ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=clouds%20and%20to%20classify%20them,LiDARs%20and%20two%20monocular%20cameras)).
       * If not using YOLO, simply sample the image at the projected location (or a small patch) and determine the dominant color (distinguish blue vs yellow vs orange by HSV ranges).
   - Handle edge cases: multiple LiDAR cones might project near each other in image (unlikely unless cones very close). If a YOLO detection doesn’t have a corresponding LiDAR point (e.g., very distant cone seen by camera but LiDAR hasn’t detected it yet), you can instantiate a new cone in the map with vision-only range estimate.
   - Publish the fused detection: a list of cones with 3D position (x,y) and classification (blue/yellow/orange). This is the core output that planning and SLAM will consume.

5. **SLAM and Mapping:**
   - Implement a SLAM node that subscribes to cone detections and to odometry (from IMU/Wheel). If using EKF SLAM:
       * Maintain state of vehicle pose (x, y, heading) and a map of cones (as landmarks in a state vector, or as separate structure if using an external mapper).
       * On each IMU/odom tick, propagate the pose.
       * On each cone detection, perform data association: match it to an existing landmark or initialize a new landmark if it’s a new cone. Then update the filter (correct step). Thrun’s book (Probabilistic Robotics) has the standard EKF SLAM equations ([The track layout of a trackdrive discipline (FSG, 2018). Blue and... | Download Scientific Diagram](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/The-track-layout-of-a-trackdrive-discipline-FSG-2018-Blue-and-yellow-cones-mark-the_fig1_343447423#:~:text=,)).
       * Over time, this will produce a map of cones in the vehicle’s coordinate system. You may want to switch to a world/inertial frame once you have a stable origin (e.g., the start position).
   - If using a graph SLAM:
       * Collect detections and poses and periodically (maybe at end of lap or every few seconds) run an optimization that adjusts the poses and landmark positions to best fit all the observations.
       * Use GTSAM or other solver to do this efficiently.
       * After optimization, broadcast an updated map of cone positions.
   - Also consider simpler mapping: since cones are static, after the first lap you could essentially have a file or structure with all cone coordinates. In subsequent laps, treat that as fixed and just localize against it.

6. **Path Planning and Obstacle Avoidance:**
   - Even though this guide focuses on perception, it’s important to close the loop. Use the cone map to generate a drivable path. A planning node can take the list of left and right cones and compute a spline path equidistant from them (the “centerline”). This can be done by pairing cones by color (left boundary = yellow, right = blue, for instance) and interpolating a smooth line mid-way.
   - Incorporate obstacle avoidance by adding a buffer around each cone. For example, create a costmap grid where each cone is inflated by, say, 0.5m, and then run a **path optimizer** (like an A* or model predictive control) to find the fastest path through the costmap from start to finish. This ensures the path doesn’t skim too close to cones.
   - For dynamic adaptation, if the map is incomplete (Autocross first lap), the planner should plan to a short horizon (to the furthest known cones) and update as new cones appear. A **receding horizon** planner or pure pursuit on the continually extending centerline works well.
   - The output of planning is a series of waypoints or a continuous curve that the control system will follow. Ensure the perception provides enough lead time – e.g., detect cones at least 5-10m ahead of the car’s current position to give the planner time to react.

7. **Integration and Testing:**
   - Integrate all nodes in ROS2 such that the LiDAR and camera nodes feed into the fusion, which feeds into SLAM, which provides input to planning. Use ROS2 launch files to bring up the entire stack. On the Jetson Orin, allocate CPU cores to heavy nodes if needed (you can set CPU affinities).
   - Test the system piecewise: first, verify LiDAR detection alone by driving the car (or moving the sensor) around some cones and visualizing that the LiDAR clusters are correctly identified. Next, test the vision detection alone with colored cones. Then test fusion by verifying that the system correctly labels each LiDAR cluster with the right color from the camera.
   - Validate the SLAM by driving in a simple known arrangement (like a square of cones) and then checking if the mapped cone positions match their real positions. If you have differential GPS or a total station, measure ground truth for a few points to quantify mapping error.

8. **Dynamic Event Specific Tuning:**
   - Configure mode-specific parameters if necessary. For example, in Acceleration mode, you might disable the SLAM map building and just do localization + simple path (straight line). In Trackdrive, load the pre-built map and skip right to localization mode for higher accuracy.
   - Ensure mode switching is smooth: after completing Autocross (mapping lap), the system should save the map and be able to reload it for Trackdrive without a reset that loses localization. One idea is to use the end of Autocross as the start of Trackdrive, continuing the pose estimation seamlessly.
   - For Skidpad, you might use a dedicated planner (like the IIT Bombay team did with a segmented pre-planned path ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=4,Skidpad%20Event)) ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=A%20predetermined%20track%20is%20followed,red%2C%20green%20%26%20magenta))) – integrate that with your perception by aligning the known skidpad map to the car’s starting position using the initial cones.

A rigorous validation strategy will ensure the perception system meets the competition requirements:

- **Offline Dataset Testing:** Record rosbag logs of sensor data from various track scenarios. Develop offline scripts to run your perception stack on this data and measure detection and localization performance. Metrics to evaluate:
  - *Cone detection precision/recall:* What fraction of cones were detected? Any false detections? This can be measured by manually labeling some frames or by cross-checking against a known map.
  - *Classification accuracy:* Percentage of cones whose color was correctly identified. Misclassifications (e.g., labeling a blue cone as yellow) should be near zero, as they can cause mapping errors.
  - *Localization error:* If you have ground truth for the vehicle path (e.g., high-precision GPS or a motion capture on a test track), compare the SLAM-estimated path to ground truth. Compute RMS error. Ideally, lateral position error should be within a few centimeters to confidently avoid cones at speed.
  - *Mapping error:* After a mapping run, compare the built map of cones to a surveyed map. The error in cone positions should be small (the AMZ team reported ~0.29 m error after their high-speed mapping ([Accurate Mapping and Planning for Autonomous Racing](https://jenjenchung.github.io/anthropomorphic/Papers/Andresen2020accurate.pdf#:~:text=show%20improved%20accuracy%20when%20driving,29%20m))). Consistency is key: if the map has slight errors but is internally consistent, the car can still navigate fine as long as it localizes in that same map.

- **Real-Time Performance Metrics:** On the Jetson, use profiling tools (like `tegrastats` or ROS2 tracing) to measure CPU/GPU usage and pipeline latency. Ensure that:
  - LiDAR processing from sensor input to output of detected cones happens well within the 100 ms interval (10 Hz) – e.g., if it takes 30 ms, that’s good.
  - Camera processing runs within the 33 ms frame time for 30 FPS (or whatever your camera FPS is).
  - No node has unsustainable load (keep CPU usage per core < 80% to avoid random delays, and GPU usage below max to allow bursts).
  - End-to-end latency (from sensing a cone to having it in the map and planner) should be low. Aim for <100 ms total, so that at 15 m/s, the car moves at most ~1.5 m in that time, which is acceptable given cone spacing. Lower is always better.

- **Track Testing:** Before competition, test the car in all event modes:
  - Set up a mock acceleration run: verify the car launches straight and stops at the right point consistently. Measure the stopping accuracy (distance overshoot/undershoot).
  - Set up a skidpad with cones: let the car run the figure-8. Check if it hits any cones or oscillates. Use timing equipment to see if the lap times are close to what a known good time is, indicating the car isn’t going overly cautious.
  - Autocross simulation: create a small unknown track and have the car do a single lap. Observe if it ever pauses or slows excessively (could indicate perception uncertainty). The car should smoothly navigate through, building the map on the fly. Afterward, inspect the generated map vs actual cone layout.
  - Trackdrive rehearsal: run multiple laps on a known track. Look for improvement in times over laps as intended. If times are inconsistent, it may hint at perception issues (like if SLAM loses localization intermittently, the car might slow down on some laps).

- **Robustness Testing:** Introduce some noise: for instance, slightly mis-position a cone or remove one to simulate a knocked-down cone. See if the system still handles it (it should, perhaps by treating the gap as a wider opening). Test in different lighting (morning sun glare vs cloudy) to ensure vision algorithms hold up – adjust camera exposure or retrain CNN if needed for these cases. Also test with other cars or people around (during competition there may be static objects at track edges like barriers or people beyond the cones) – your perception should ignore non-cone objects or at least not confuse them for cones.

For example, if for some reason the perception stops publishing updates (maybe a node crash), the vehicle’s watchdog should engage the brakes. This isn’t part of perception per se, but a critical part of the driverless system safety.

Throughout development, maintain a **feedback loop**: use the validation results to improve the system. If you find, say, that the LiDAR sometimes merges two close cones into one cluster (thus “missing” a cone), you can tweak the clustering distance or use the camera to help distinguish them. If the classification struggles in low light, consider adding LED illumination or using LiDAR intensity as backup.

# LiDAR-Only Perception for Formula SAE Driverless Racing

Developing a LiDAR-only perception system for an autonomous Formula SAE vehicle requires accurate cone detection/classification, precise SLAM for localization, and a fast, low-latency pipeline. This guide outlines state-of-the-art techniques and practical steps in four key areas: cone classification using LiDAR intensity, high-accuracy SLAM with cone landmarks, low-latency pipeline design (targeted for NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin), and implementation guidelines for a robust LiDAR-only perception stack.

## 1. Cone Classification Using LiDAR Intensity and Reflectivity

**Distinguishing Cone Colors via Intensity Patterns:** Formula Student driverless tracks are delineated by traffic cones of different colors (typically **blue**, **yellow**, and occasionally **orange**), marking track boundaries and special zones. Even though LiDAR does not capture visible color, it provides **intensity/reflectivity** returns that vary with surface properties. Competition cones are known to exhibit distinct intensity patterns – for example, *yellow cones often yield a bright-dark-bright intensity pattern along their height, while blue cones show a dark-bright-dark pattern* ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=From%20the%20image%20above%2C%20we,differentiate%20between%20these%20cone%20types)) ([](http://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.05150#:~:text=According%20to%20the%20rule%20book,grayscale%20image%20of%20the%20cone)). These patterns arise from the cones’ physical design (e.g. colored sections or reflective stripes) and material reflectance. State-of-the-art methods leverage these intensity gradients to differentiate cone colors. For instance, one team noted that according to the FSG rulebook the *yellow cone has a yellow-black-yellow physical pattern and the blue cone a blue-white-blue pattern, which translates into different LiDAR intensity gradients vertically* ([](http://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.05150#:~:text=According%20to%20the%20rule%20book,grayscale%20image%20of%20the%20cone)). By analyzing the intensity distribution of LiDAR points on a cone, the system can classify its color without any camera.

**Intensity Normalization and Calibration:** Raw LiDAR intensity values depend on range, incidence angle, and material reflectivity. To reliably use intensity for color classification, it’s important to **normalize or calibrate** these readings so that a blue cone at 5 m and at 15 m produce comparable intensity patterns. Generally, intensity should be corrected for distance and angle effects ([Lidar Intensity Information: A Review of Applications and Processing Techniques - LiDAR News](https://lidarnews.com/articles/lidar-intensity-information-a-review-of-applications-and-processing-techniques/#:~:text=,a%20histogram%20matching%20or%20normalization)). Many LiDAR sensors (e.g. Ouster) provide a **reflectivity** measurement which is a calibrated intensity intended to be independent of range and incidence angle ([Lidar Intensity Information: A Review of Applications and Processing Techniques - LiDAR News](https://lidarnews.com/articles/lidar-intensity-information-a-review-of-applications-and-processing-techniques/#:~:text=,different%20systems%2C%20operated%20with%20different)). Using this reflectivity data (when available) is advisable for robust classification, as it yields more consistent values across different environments and distances. If only raw intensity is available, an empirical calibration can be applied – for example, adjusting intensity by a distance-squared factor or referencing a known reflectance target. The goal is to produce intensity/reflectivity values that reflect material properties (the cone’s color/reflectance) rather than how far or at what angle the cone was hit by the laser. This ensures that classification thresholds or models won’t break under different lighting or track conditions. (Notably, LiDAR intensity is largely unaffected by ambient light since it’s an active sensor; however, strong sunlight can introduce sensor noise, which the sensor’s ambient readings can help filter if needed ([What is the range of intensity and reflectivity? · Issue #128 · ouster-lidar/ouster-sdk · GitHub](https://github.com/ouster-lidar/ouster_example/issues/128#:~:text=Intensity%20returned%20from%20the%20OS,with%20far%20too%20less%20contrast)).)

**Color Classification Techniques:** Two broad approaches exist for classifying cone color from intensity data: **rule-based pattern recognition** and **machine-learning classification**. In simple cases, one can design a rule-based classifier by extracting features from the intensity returns of each cone’s point cloud cluster. For example, you might divide the vertical extent of the cone’s points into three parts (top, middle, bottom) and compare the intensity averages. A blue cone tends to have a weaker-stronger-weaker intensity profile (due to a white reflective band in the middle) whereas a yellow cone shows the inverse pattern ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=From%20the%20image%20above%2C%20we,differentiate%20between%20these%20cone%20types)). Such heuristic classifiers require tuning but can work if the patterns are consistent. Additionally, different colors have inherently different reflectivity at the LiDAR’s infrared wavelength – e.g. tests have shown **orange** paint can return much higher intensity than darker colors at the same distance ([](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/autosec2022_23039_paper.pdf#:~:text=orange%20has%20the%20highest%20average,from%20295%20to%209)) ([](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/autosec2022_23039_paper.pdf#:~:text=Take,In%20the)). By measuring the typical reflectivity of blue vs. yellow cone material (and reflective tape, if present), one can set intensity thresholds to differentiate them. For instance, one study found that *darker colors produce lower average LiDAR intensity, whereas more reflective (brighter) colors return higher intensity* ([](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/autosec2022_23039_paper.pdf#:~:text=Take,In%20the)) – indicating that, after calibration, a yellow cone (bright plastic) might consistently give higher reflectivity than a blue cone (darker plastic) at equal range. These statistical differences can feed into a simple classifier (like a logistic regression or decision tree) using features such as mean intensity and intensity contrast within the cluster.

**Machine Learning Models:** The more robust approach is to use a learning-based model to classify cones from intensity data. A common state-of-the-art solution is to treat the LiDAR intensity returns as a sort of grayscale “image” of the cone and apply a CNN (convolutional neural network). For example, teams have projected each detected cone’s point cloud onto a **32×32 intensity image** (by spherical projection or bounding-box projection) and trained a CNN to output cone color ([](http://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.05150#:~:text=is%20leveraged%20in%20the%20cone,The%20pixel)). This was done by AMZ Driverless using a network with four conv layers and five fully-connected layers, outputting probabilities for blue, yellow, or unknown ([](http://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.05150#:~:text=is%20leveraged%20in%20the%20cone,to%20the%20center%20of%20the)). Similarly, the Melbourne MUR team cropped an intensity image around each cone and used a small CNN to classify it ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=A%20simple%20convolutional%20neural%20network,32%20x%2032)). These networks automatically learn the intensity patterns (bright/dark band locations) rather than relying on manually set thresholds. Even with tiny models, high inference speeds are attainable – e.g. MUR converted their model to TensorRT on the Jetson and achieved **<1 ms inference per cone** (batching up to 50 cones) ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=Before%20deployment%2C%20the%20classifier%20model,size%20of%20up%20to%2050)). This means the classification step adds virtually no latency, which is crucial in a racing context. When using such models, dataset collection and augmentation are important: one should gather LiDAR scans of cones under various conditions (indoor, outdoor, different distances) to train the network. If collecting real-world data is limited, simulation can help (some teams simulate LiDAR returns for colored cones to generate training data). Data augmentation (random rotations, noise, intensity scaling) can also improve model robustness to sensor variations ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=However%2C%20due%20to%20the%20limitation,the%20unusually%20high%20validation%20accuracy)). Because LiDAR point clouds are sparse, ensure the model is valid for the sensor’s range – e.g. a 64-beam LiDAR provided enough vertical resolution to classify cones up to ~12 m away in one case ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=A%20major%20limitation%20of%20this,9m)), whereas a 16-beam LiDAR only allowed ~4–5 m classification range before the cone’s image was too small ([](http://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.05150#:~:text=However%2C%20the%20sparsity%20of%20the,for%20a%20distinction%20between%20the)). In practice, the vehicle will get within that range, so the system can mark far detections as “unknown color” initially and classify once closer.

**Robustness Across Conditions:** To maintain classification accuracy in varying conditions, it’s recommended to **calibrate the intensity** on your specific LiDAR. Perform tests by placing cones at known distances (5 m, 10 m, 15 m) and recording intensity; adjust your normalization so that a given cone yields a consistent intensity value regardless of range. Also be mindful of **sensor-specific settings**: some LiDARs have reflectivity mode toggles or auto-exposure parameters. Use a consistent setting that provides stable intensity readings (for example, Ouster’s dual returns or single return mode can affect intensity). If using the Ouster LiDAR, take advantage of their SDK: Ouster provides an `img_node` utility showing how to obtain images for intensity, reflectivity, and ambient data from the sensor stream ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=time,intensity%2C%20reflectivity%2C%20and%20ambient%20noise)). This can jump-start the development of an intensity-based classifier by giving a ready-made cylindrical projection. In summary, by normalizing intensity data and leveraging its inherent patterns (manually or via a CNN), a LiDAR-only system can **reliably distinguish blue vs. yellow cones** (left vs. right boundary) and identify orange cones – all critical for understanding the track layout ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=While%20the%20previous%20LiDAR%20pipeline,of%20the%20race%20track%20respectively)). This classification becomes an important input to the planning algorithm, since the vehicle must know which side of the track a cone belongs to.

## 2. High-Accuracy SLAM with LiDAR Landmarks

Accurate **simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM)** is essential for autonomous racing since no external GPS is used. The car must build a map of the track (cone positions) and localize itself within that map using only onboard sensors. Here, each cone detected (with its color label) serves as a **landmark** for SLAM. The SLAM system must handle high speeds and quick maneuvers, so it should be both precise and computationally efficient. Common approaches include **EKF-SLAM**, **Graph-SLAM**, and **Particle Filter-based SLAM** (e.g. FastSLAM). Each has pros and cons in this racing context:

- **EKF-SLAM (Extended Kalman Filter SLAM):** This method maintains a state vector comprising the vehicle pose (x, y, orientation) and the positions of all detected cones, with a covariance matrix tracking uncertainty. When the car drives, it uses a motion model (typically from wheel odometry and IMU) to predict its new pose, and when a cone is observed, it updates the state and refines both the car’s pose and the cone map. EKF-SLAM has been a popular choice in Formula Student due to its real-time capability and simplicity ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=The%20Extended%20Kalman%20Filter%20,the%20motion%20update%20to%20continue)). It can be optimized for speed by reducing state size (e.g. “forgetting” distant cones that are no longer relevant) and by efficient data structures for the covariance update. In practice, teams have run EKF-SLAM successfully with dozens of landmarks in real time on embedded hardware ([A Performance Comparison of SLAM-Algorithms for Formula Student Autonomous Driving | springerprofessional.de](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/a-performance-comparison-of-slam-algorithms-for-formula-student-/50428406#:~:text=SLAM%2C%20FastSLAM%20and%20GraphSLAM,datasets)). One advantage of EKF-SLAM is that it provides **direct data association** and fuses information on the fly, making immediate corrections to the estimated pose whenever a cone is observed. However, a standard EKF can become inconsistent or overly confident if the vehicle traverses a loop (because the linearized update may not fully correct a long-term drift). Despite that, in the relatively small, closed tracks of FSAE, EKF-SLAM has been shown to perform very well – in fact, one comparative study found that a tuned EKF-SLAM outperformed both Graph-SLAM and FastSLAM in terms of vehicle and landmark position accuracy as well as computational load ([A Performance Comparison of SLAM-Algorithms for Formula Student Autonomous Driving | springerprofessional.de](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/a-performance-comparison-of-slam-algorithms-for-formula-student-/50428406#:~:text=SLAM%2C%20FastSLAM%20and%20GraphSLAM,datasets)). This was likely due to the filter’s ability to continuously correct drift and the relatively limited scale of the environment.

- **Graph-SLAM (Graphical SLAM / Optimization-based SLAM):** Graph-SLAM approaches (such as smoothing and mapping) record each pose of the vehicle as a node in a graph and each landmark observation as a constraint (edge) between the pose node and a landmark node. After collecting numerous observations (e.g. over a lap), the method solves a global optimization problem (typically via least-squares) to find the arrangement of all poses and landmarks that best fits the measurements. The advantage is **high global consistency** – effectively a loop closure is naturally handled by the graph optimization, yielding very accurate maps. A top-performing team reported using Graph-SLAM to map cones with a root-mean-square error under **15 cm** while racing at 70+ km/h ([(PDF) The Software Stack That Won the Formula Student Driverless Competition](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364515298_The_Software_Stack_That_Won_the_Formula_Student_Driverless_Competition#:~:text=physical%20limits%20of%20the%20car,lane%20boundaries%20using%20Delaunay%20triangulation)). This accuracy is excellent for maintaining a precise racing line. The trade-off is that Graph-SLAM can be computationally heavier; it often requires iterative solvers (like Gauss-Newton) and can grow in time complexity as the map expands. However, given a typical track (~50–100 cones), modern libraries (g2o, GTSAM, etc.) can optimize the graph quickly, especially if formulated sparsely. Some teams run Graph-SLAM in a slightly **deferred** manner: e.g. do quick pose estimates during driving (using odometry or EKF) and then periodically (or at the end of the lap) perform a full graph optimization to refine the map. The refined map can then be fed back for subsequent laps or for path planning. Graph-SLAM is also amenable to **SLAM with known correspondences** – if each cone is uniquely identified by color and some ordering, data association is simpler. In practice, cones are not uniquely identifiable by color alone (since many cones share the same color), but the structure (left vs right side) can be exploited to match observations to map. Graph-SLAM thrives when **loop closure** occurs – e.g. when the car approaches the finish line and sees cones first observed at the start, the optimizer will adjust the entire map (and the vehicle’s past trajectory) to be consistent, something an EKF would adjust only gradually.

- **Particle Filter SLAM (FastSLAM):** Particle filters represent the vehicle’s pose uncertainty with a set of particles (hypotheses), each carrying its own map of landmarks. FastSLAM 2.0, for example, uses particles for the vehicle pose and an EKF for the landmarks within each particle. The benefit is the ability to handle non-linear motion models and ambiguous data associations by maintaining multiple hypotheses. In a racing scenario with clear line-of-sight cones, ambiguity is usually low, so a large number of particles (and thus heavy computation) may not be necessary. Indeed, experiments suggest FastSLAM doesn’t outperform simpler methods in this context ([A Performance Comparison of SLAM-Algorithms for Formula Student Autonomous Driving | springerprofessional.de](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/a-performance-comparison-of-slam-algorithms-for-formula-student-/50428406#:~:text=SLAM%2C%20FastSLAM%20and%20GraphSLAM,datasets)). Particle filters can suffer from **degeneracy** (many particles get low weight and are effectively wasted) if the localization uncertainty is not very high to begin with (and in our case, good wheel/IMU odometry keeps uncertainty modest). Thus, FastSLAM’s benefit might not justify its computational cost here. Most FS teams lean towards EKF or graph-based methods rather than pure particle SLAM, due to the latter’s complexity in tuning and processing.

**Real-Time Optimizations:** Regardless of SLAM approach, certain optimizations are critical for a racing environment:
- **High-frequency Odometry Fusion:** Use **sensor fusion** of LiDAR and other onboard sensors to improve accuracy. Typically, an IMU (inertial measurement unit) and wheel speed encoders are fused to provide a smooth odometry estimate of the car’s motion (this can be through a separate vehicle state estimator or within the SLAM filter). This odometry is used as the prediction step for SLAM. For example, the EKF-SLAM in one design uses the car’s IMU and wheel encoder data for the motion update, then corrects with cone observations in the measurement update ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=competition%20tracks,10%20shows%20this%20parallel%20flow)). The IMU fills in high-frequency pose changes (so the car’s pose is always up-to-date even between LiDAR frames) while LiDAR landmarks prevent drift.
- **Efficient Data Association:** Using cones as point landmarks means the SLAM algorithm must decide which observed cone corresponds to which previously mapped cone. Robust data association is crucial – an incorrect association (confusing one cone for another) can severely corrupt the map. In real time, a common strategy is **Nearest-Neighbor (NN)** association: associate each observed cone with the nearest cone in the current map within a reasonable radius. This is fast (O(N) per observation) but can fail if cones are very closely spaced or if the vehicle’s position estimate has error. More advanced techniques like **Joint Compatibility Branch and Bound (JCBB)** evaluate the compatibility of a set of associations jointly, searching for the combination that best fits the vehicle’s pose uncertainty ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=Cones%20that%20are%20seen%20for,interpretations%20by%20systematically%20examining%20the)). JCBB is more robust to spurious matches (it won’t pair a cone that doesn’t fit the expected relative geometry), but it is computationally heavier (exponential in the number of observations, though cones per frame are not huge). In practice, many teams start with a simple nearest-neighbor approach with gating (i.e., reject matches that are too far or angle-mismatched) ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=Cones%20that%20are%20seen%20for,interpretations%20by%20systematically%20examining%20the)). If an observation can’t be matched, it initializes a new landmark in the map. Tuning the gating threshold is important: it should be large enough to account for pose uncertainty but small enough to avoid confusing adjacent cones. Note that using the **cone color** can greatly aid association: a blue cone observation should only match a mapped blue cone, never a yellow, reducing the search space.
- **Map Management:** In a dynamic racing setting, you may only need the local portion of the map for immediate localization. Some SLAM implementations keep a sliding window of active landmarks (e.g., only cones within 30 m). However, for FS tracks, it’s usually manageable to keep all cones in memory since the total number is limited. What is important is to manage the computation per update – an EKF with 100 landmarks means a 100×100 covariance matrix; update steps scale with O(n^2) in naive implementation, which at 100 landmarks and, say, 5 observed at a time, is fine on modern hardware. If using Graph-SLAM, the number of variables is also limited (each cone plus each keyframe pose). As an example, the KIT team’s GraphSLAM handled ~70 cones and a similar number of poses with <15 cm error ([(PDF) The Software Stack That Won the Formula Student Driverless Competition](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364515298_The_Software_Stack_That_Won_the_Formula_Student_Driverless_Competition#:~:text=physical%20limits%20of%20the%20car,lane%20boundaries%20using%20Delaunay%20triangulation)), demonstrating feasibility.
- **Loop Closure Handling:** If using EKF-SLAM, a “loop closure” (re-observing a cone after a long absence) just comes in as another measurement – the filter will adjust that cone’s position and the vehicle pose slightly, but large loops can be challenging. With Graph-SLAM, loop closure is naturally integrated; one just continues to add observations to the graph and the solver will distribute the error. In practice, completing the first lap provides a complete map of cones. For subsequent laps (e.g. in endurance event), the car can localize purely against this map (this becomes ** localization** on a known map, which can be done with a particle filter or an EKF in localization-mode). Some teams effectively switch from SLAM to localization once the map is built, to reduce computation and fix the map. Others keep SLAM running to correct any slight map inaccuracies continuously.

**Sensor Fusion without GPS:** Aside from feeding wheel/IMU into SLAM, another technique is combining **LiDAR odometry** with landmark SLAM. LiDAR odometry (e.g. scan matching between consecutive point clouds) can provide a short-term pose delta that is very precise. For example, algorithms like LOAM (LiDAR Odometry and Mapping) split the problem into a fast odometry estimation and a slower mapping refinement ([High-Precision SLAM Based on the Tight Coupling of Dual Lidar ...](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/3/939#:~:text=,to%20reduce%20the%20computation)). In a cones scenario, one could run a scan-to-scan or scan-to-map ICP (Iterative Closest Point) alignment on the cone point clouds themselves. However, since cones are sparse and symmetric, pure ICP may not be as robust as feature-rich environments. A more tailored approach is to use the **cone map** in a localization filter: as the car moves, predict its pose (via IMU), then match the pattern of observed cones against the map (finding the best pose that minimizes distance to the nearest mapped cones). This is effectively what an EKF/Graph-SLAM does internally. Another fusion approach is to integrate **visual odometry** if a camera is present, but here we assume LiDAR-only – though it’s worth noting that some teams run parallel perception pipelines (camera and LiDAR) for redundancy ([](http://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.05150#:~:text=which%20calls%20for%20redundancy%20in,A%20LiDAR%20was)) ([](http://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.05150#:~:text=The%20second%20sensor%20modality%20is,5)). Without GPS, the combination of wheel encoders (to constrain drift in distance), IMU (to stabilize orientation and short-term motion), and LiDAR landmark updates (to correct global position) has proven sufficient for maintaining accuracy over an entire autocross lap or endurance run.

**Comparison and Recommendation:** In summary, **EKF-SLAM** is generally sufficient and easier to implement for real-time cone-based SLAM. It offers low latency and was shown to be very competitive in accuracy for FS tracks ([A Performance Comparison of SLAM-Algorithms for Formula Student Autonomous Driving | springerprofessional.de](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/a-performance-comparison-of-slam-algorithms-for-formula-student-/50428406#:~:text=SLAM%2C%20FastSLAM%20and%20GraphSLAM,datasets)). **Graph-SLAM** can yield higher accuracy and is more elegant for handling loops, but requires a bit more computation and a good backend solver (g2o, etc.) – it’s a good choice if you plan to push for maximum precision (sub-10 cm) and can afford slight delays for optimization. **FastSLAM/particle filter** approaches are less common in this domain due to their complexity, but could be considered if you expect multi-modal uncertainties (e.g., if the car could start in an unknown location and need to figure out where on the track it is – which is usually not the case, as starting position is known). In any case, whichever SLAM method is used, careful tuning of noise parameters and thorough testing on a known track (measuring the mapped cone positions vs. ground truth) should be done. Many teams validate their SLAM by driving the car in a controlled pattern and seeing if the cones in the map line up with their actual surveyed positions within the required tolerance (e.g. <0.2 m). With only LiDAR and no GPS, achieving **centimeter-level localization** is challenging but demonstrated to be feasible with optimized algorithms ([(PDF) The Software Stack That Won the Formula Student Driverless Competition](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364515298_The_Software_Stack_That_Won_the_Formula_Student_Driverless_Competition#:~:text=physical%20limits%20of%20the%20car,lane%20boundaries%20using%20Delaunay%20triangulation)), enabling the vehicle to confidently navigate at speed between the cones.

## 3. Low-Latency Perception Pipeline

At racing speeds, **low latency** in the perception stack is critical – any significant delay between sensing and actuation can mean the difference between hitting a cone and avoiding it. The perception pipeline (from LiDAR scan to output of cone positions and colors) must operate faster than the LiDAR’s frame rate, ideally with margin to spare. For instance, if using a 20 Hz LiDAR (50 ms per frame), the entire pipeline should well complete in <50 ms. A team in the autonomous racing domain explicitly built their LiDAR pipeline to run *“with a latency lower than the sensor refresh rate”* ([LiDAR-Based Vehicle Detection and Tracking for Autonomous Racing](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14502v1#:~:text=In%20this%20work%2C%20we%20build,the%20availability%20of%20labeled%20data)), ensuring real-time performance. Achieving this on the **NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin** requires leveraging its computational strengths (multiple CPU cores and a powerful GPU) and using efficient algorithms. Here are strategies to design a low-latency LiDAR perception pipeline:

- **Real-Time Processing and Parallelism:** Structure the pipeline so that tasks can run in parallel whenever possible, rather than strictly sequentially. For example, while the current LiDAR scan is being processed for clustering, the next scan can be loading or a previous stage (like ground filtering) can be running on new data concurrently. Using separate threads or asynchronous ROS callbacks can help overlap I/O and computation. One effective pattern is a **pipeline with double buffering** – as soon as one LiDAR frame is acquired, send it off for processing; when the next frame arrives, start processing it even if the previous frame’s processing hasn’t fully completed (assuming different stages). In an EKF-SLAM context, one team parallelized the prediction and update steps: the motion prediction runs continuously at high frequency, while the measurement update (incorporating new cone observations) runs in another thread, preventing delays in pose output ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=location%20and%20colour%20information%20from,of%20motion%20and%20measurement%20update)). This kind of parallel flow ensured that the vehicle pose estimate was always fresh for the controller ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=The%20Extended%20Kalman%20Filter%20,step%20in%20the%20background%2C%20thus)). On Jetson Orin, which has 12 CPU cores, one can dedicate certain cores to specific nodes or threads (e.g. a dedicated core for LiDAR driver and pre-processing, another for clustering, etc.). **ROS2 executors** can be configured for multi-threading to take advantage of this. If multiple LiDARs were used, processing each in parallel would be beneficial (PoliMOVE’s Indy AV car processed three LiDARs in parallel threads) ([LiDAR-Based Vehicle Detection and Tracking for Autonomous Racing](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14502v1#:~:text=with%20Point%20Cloud%20Segmentation%2C%20where,Next%2C%20we%20estimate)), but for a single LiDAR, parallelizing internal stages is the focus.

- **GPU Acceleration:** The Orin’s GPU can massively speed up certain perception tasks. **Neural network inference** is a prime candidate – as noted, using TensorRT on Orin allowed cone classification in 1 ms ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=Before%20deployment%2C%20the%20classifier%20model,size%20of%20up%20to%2050)), which is essentially real-time. If a deep learning model is used anywhere (e.g. a point cloud segmentation CNN or a classifier), convert it to use TensorRT or a lightweight runtime so it runs on the GPU with FP16 or INT8 precision as appropriate. Beyond ML, GPU can also accelerate point cloud operations. While the classic PCL library is CPU-bound, there exist approaches to do **range image ground removal and clustering on GPU**. For instance, the approach by PoliMOVE converted the point cloud to a range image and performed ground filtering and connected-component clustering in image space – this process is highly parallelizable and was likely implemented to run efficiently (potentially on GPU) ([LiDAR-Based Vehicle Detection and Tracking for Autonomous Racing](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14502v1#:~:text=with%20Point%20Cloud%20Segmentation%2C%20where,Next%2C%20we%20estimate)). If writing custom CUDA kernels is feasible, operations like segmenting ground (by scanning for the lowest points in each column of the range image) and clustering (by flood-fill on the range image or by hashing point coordinates into a grid) can be done with thread parallelism. NVIDIA’s CUDA libraries (thrust, etc.) or even OpenCL could be used to speed up parts of the pipeline. As a simpler step, you can use **pcl::VoxelGrid** filtering with NVIDIA’s NPP library for fast downsampling, though PCL doesn’t natively integrate NPP – writing a custom downsampling on GPU might be worth if point clouds are very dense.

- **Efficient Ground Removal:** Removing ground points quickly reduces the data volume for subsequent steps. A variety of methods exist, and choosing one that balances speed and accuracy is key. The ground can be thought of as a dominant plane. A common approach is to use **RANSAC plane fitting** to identify the ground plane in the cloud and remove inliers. PCL has `pcl::SACSegmentation` which can do this reasonably fast if the plane is obvious. However, RANSAC can be overkill if we *know* the ground is relatively flat and the LiDAR is mounted roughly horizontal – simpler approaches can exploit the structured nature of the scan. For example, many automotive LiDAR ground filters use a **scan-line method**: for each horizontal angular sector (or each LiDAR ring), compare the elevation of points and label points as ground if they are at a consistent low height relative to the vehicle. Another approach: transform the point cloud to the vehicle’s base frame and apply a height threshold (say, drop all points with z < -0.3 m relative to the LiDAR) – this is very fast and can work if the vehicle is on level ground and cones sit higher than that cutoff. But it might fail on uneven terrain or if the LiDAR tilts during acceleration/braking. Thus, many teams implement a hybrid: first do a coarse filter by height, then a refined ground detection. **Range-image based ground removal** is especially efficient: one method is to project the LiDAR points onto a cylindrical range image (where each image row corresponds to a LiDAR channel/scan ring). Ground points will appear along the lower part of each column in this image. By scanning each column for the first significant jump in height, one can distinguish ground from a rising object. The PoliMOVE team’s approach effectively uses a horizontal smoothing filter in the range image to identify ground pixels ([LiDAR-Based Vehicle Detection and Tracking for Autonomous Racing](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14502v1#:~:text=Ground%20Removal%20step%2C%20by%20applying,merging%20of%20connected%20components%20belonging)). Such image-based methods can run in O(N) time (N = number of points) and be easily parallelized. Regardless of method, tune the ground filter to be slightly conservative (it’s better to leave a few ground points in the cloud than to mistakenly remove the bottom of a cone). After ground removal, you might reduce the point cloud to, say, 10–20% of its original size, focusing only on obstacle points.

- **Fast Clustering of Cone Points:** Once ground points are gone, what remains (in an FSAE track) are mostly discrete clusters of cone returns. A naive Euclidean clustering on all remaining points can be time-consuming for large clouds – the PCL Euclidean clustering algorithm has to do neighborhood searches which, if not optimized, scale poorly with point count. As noted in one study, **PCL’s Euclidean clustering becomes unsuitable for online use with large point clouds unless heavy downsampling is applied** ([LiDAR-Based Vehicle Detection and Tracking for Autonomous Racing](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14502v1#:~:text=Beyond%20Ground%20Removal%2C%20the%20most,input%20downsampling%2C%20and%20subsequent%20information)). We want to avoid losing too much information, so instead of downsampling excessively, consider alternative clustering strategies:
  - Use the **range image connectivity**: After ground removal using a range image, you can reuse that image to cluster points. Simply perform a connected-component labeling on the binary image of “obstacle points”. This method inherently respects Euclidean distance (because points that are adjacent in the range image and not separated by a large depth discontinuity are part of the same object). It’s much faster than kd-tree clustering on the raw point set, since the LiDAR’s angular structure is exploited. The output is a set of pixels belonging to each cluster, which can be converted back to 3D point indices.
  - If you prefer working in Cartesian space, **spatial hashing** can speed up neighbor searches. For example, divide the environment into a grid (e.g. 0.5 m cells) and use that to seed clustering: points in the same or adjacent grid cells are candidates for the same cluster, reducing the search range. KD-trees (used by PCL) are already quite efficient for nearest neighbor queries, but they can still bog down with tens of thousands of points. On Orin, the clustering might still be CPU-bound, so profile it. If it’s a bottleneck, one approach is to limit the scope: you know cones have a certain maximum radius (~0.2 m). Thus, you could set the clustering tolerance to ~0.3 m – this means the algorithm will not try to link points farther apart than that. This both improves accuracy (adjacent cones won’t merge) and limits computation.
  - **Downsampling smartly:** It’s often effective to downsample just enough to merge redundant points on the same object but not so much that you lose points from small objects. For instance, a **voxel grid of 5 cm** could be applied *after* removing ground. Points on a cone, which is a small object, will mostly survive (since the cone might span, say, a 30 cm height and the returns cluster there). But extraneous redundant points (like multiple returns on the flat ground or on the same cone surface) might compress. This can reduce point count significantly without sacrificing cone detection. Remember that too aggressive downsampling (e.g. 20 cm voxels) could drop a small cone entirely if it only had a few returns.
  - If you have many cores, consider splitting the cloud into sectors (e.g. split by angle into front, left, right, back quadrants) and running clustering in parallel on each sector’s points, since cones in widely separated directions won’t cluster together. This can yield nearly 4× speedup on 4 threads, for example.
  
  After clustering, you’ll have clusters each representing a cone candidate. The number of clusters per frame is relatively low (perhaps 0 to 10 at any instant, depending on track density), so subsequent steps (classification and SLAM update) operate on just these few objects, which is very fast.

- **Minimizing Overall Latency:** Every millisecond counts in high-speed autonomy. Some additional tips to trim latency:
  - **Avoid unnecessary copies and conversions:** Using ROS, try to keep the data in the most usable format. For example, ROS2 allows zero-copy intra-process communication. If your LiDAR driver publishes a point cloud message, and your processing node is in the same process, use loaned messages or shared pointers to avoid copying the large cloud. Also, when converting to PCL format or Eigen, be mindful of copy overhead.
  - **Profiling and tuning:** Profile each pipeline stage (you can insert timers or use NVIDIA’s Nsight Systems to measure GPU/CPU usage). If ground removal takes 5 ms, clustering 20 ms, classification 2 ms, etc., you know where to focus. Often, clustering or a naive step like iterating through points in Python can be the culprit – ensure performance-critical code is in C++ and optimized.
  - **Leverage Orin’s strengths:** The Jetson Orin AGX has very powerful **AI accelerators (NVIDIA GPU)** and decent CPU. Offload what you can to GPU and use CPU in parallel for tasks that are harder to GPU-accelerate (like running the EKF state estimation might stay on CPU while the GPU handles point cloud crunching). The Orin also supports **FPGA/DLA** cores (depending on model) which can run certain neural nets with even lower power, but most use the GPU directly.
  - **Use proper data structures:** If using C++ for custom code, prefer arrays and Eigen structures over STL vectors of small objects for point data to keep cache coherence. If using PCL, use their structures which are fairly optimized.
  - **Consider output rate vs. input rate:** It may not be necessary to process every single LiDAR frame if your control loop can handle a slightly lower update rate with interpolation. However, skipping frames is generally avoided in racing – instead, ensure you really process each frame within the next frame’s arrival.
  
As a real example, the MUR team reported their LiDAR detection+classification pipeline ran in ~50 ms per iteration in indoor tests (20 Hz) ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=During%20indoor%20testing%2C%20the%20pipeline,fully%20assess%20the%20maximum%20throughout)). The Indy Autonomously-driven racecars processed three 40 Hz LiDARs and still achieved real-time detection and tracking ([LiDAR-Based Vehicle Detection and Tracking for Autonomous Racing](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14502v1#:~:text=In%20this%20work%2C%20we%20build,the%20availability%20of%20labeled%20data)) by heavy parallelization and optimized code. With a single modern LiDAR and the Jetson Orin, achieving **20–50 Hz perception update** is quite feasible. The key is to use the **GPU for heavy lifting**, parallelize tasks (think of pipeline stages as a factory assembly line rather than one worker doing all in sequence), and streamline algorithms (remove points that don’t matter early, and cluster efficiently). By minimizing perception delay, the vehicle’s control decisions are always based on the most current information, allowing it to safely drive closer to the limits of the track. Low latency also helps in fast state estimation for SLAM – less lag between sensing a cone and incorporating it into the map means more accurate localization for high-speed maneuvers.

## 4. Implementation Guide (Step-by-Step)

This section provides a practical guide to implementing the LiDAR-only perception system, from sensor setup to software frameworks and testing. It assumes the use of ROS (Robot Operating System) for integration, C++/Python for coding, and the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin as the compute platform.

**1. Sensor Setup and Calibration:**
   - **LiDAR Selection & Mounting:** Use a 360° 3D LiDAR (common choices are Velodyne VLP-16, Ouster OS1-64, etc.). Mount the LiDAR such that it has an unobstructed view around the vehicle. A higher placement (e.g. on a roll hoop or roof) reduces occlusion by other cones ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=1,LiDAR%20above%20the%20driver%20headrest)), but adhere to competition rules on sensor placement. Ensure the LiDAR is rigidly secured to avoid vibrations. 
   - **Calibration:** Calibrate the LiDAR extrinsics with respect to the vehicle frame. This can be as simple as measuring the LiDAR’s height above ground and its offset from the car’s center. Also record the mounting angle (if tilted). Many teams mount the LiDAR flat and centered for symmetry. If the LiDAR is tilted, account for that angle in ground removal algorithms.
   - If using additional sensors (IMU, wheel encoders), perform sensor calibration (e.g., extrinsic calibration of IMU orientation to LiDAR frame) so that all sensor data can be fused. Time synchronization is also important: use the Jetson’s PTP or ROS2 time to sync messages from IMU and LiDAR.

**2. Software Framework and Architecture:**
   - It’s recommended to use **ROS2** (the latest ROS) for modular design and easy integration. The perception system can be broken into multiple ROS2 nodes or components: a LiDAR driver node, a perception node (for filtering, clustering, classification), and a SLAM node for localization. ROS2’s publish-subscribe architecture will handle data flow between them, and you can take advantage of ROS tools (RViz for visualization, rosbag for recording test data).
   - The core libraries to utilize include **PCL (Point Cloud Library)** for point cloud processing (filters, segmentation) and **Eigen** for linear algebra (useful in SLAM math). These are C++ libraries that integrate well with ROS2. For GPU acceleration, you might integrate CUDA kernels or use frameworks like **OpenMP** for parallel CPU code.
   - For machine learning, if implementing the cone classifier as a CNN, use **PyTorch or TensorFlow** for training offline, then export the model (ONNX format) and deploy with **NVIDIA TensorRT** on the Jetson for runtime inference ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=Before%20deployment%2C%20the%20classifier%20model,size%20of%20up%20to%2050)). This gives you maximum throughput on Orin.
   - **Code architecture:** Aim for a clear separation of concerns. For example, within the perception node, you might have these stages: (a) Input cloud callback -> (b) ground filter -> (c) clustering -> (d) per-cluster classification -> (e) publish detected cones. Each stage could be a function, and you can chain them. This makes it easy to unit test each part (e.g., feed a point cloud to the ground filter and check output).
   - Use asynchronous communication between perception and SLAM. The perception node can publish a list of detected cones (with positions in the car frame and color labels) at, say, 20 Hz. The SLAM node subscribes to this and integrates the observations. By decoupling them, each can run at its own pace, and the overall system becomes more resilient.
   - **Graph-SLAM framework:** If you choose Graph-SLAM, consider using an existing library like **g2o or GTSAM** for the back-end optimization, as implemented by some winning teams ([(PDF) The Software Stack That Won the Formula Student Driverless Competition](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364515298_The_Software_Stack_That_Won_the_Formula_Student_Driverless_Competition#:~:text=,%E2%80%9CG2o%3A%20A%20general%20frame%20work)). If EKF-SLAM, you can either code it from scratch using Eigen (it’s manageable for low-dimensional filters) or use a robotics library (e.g. MRPT has an EKF SLAM implementation ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=3,16))). Ensure the SLAM is tightly integrated with odometry sources (likely a separate node publishing fused odometry from IMU and wheels).
   - The entire software stack that won FSG driverless in 2021 was written in ROS C++ and tested both in simulation and on track ([(PDF) The Software Stack That Won the Formula Student Driverless Competition](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364515298_The_Software_Stack_That_Won_the_Formula_Student_Driverless_Competition#:~:text=realized%20in%20ROS%20C%2B%2B%20and,on%20the%20actual%20race%20track)), which underlines that using these standard tools is sufficient to achieve high performance.

**3. LiDAR Pre-processing Steps:**
   1. **Ground Plane Removal:** Implement ground filtering as described in Section 3. You can start simple: for example, use PCL’s `segmentGroundFromLidarData` if available (MATLAB and Autoware have similar functions). Otherwise, a custom RANSAC: use `pcl::SACSegmentation` with model type `SACMODEL_PLANE` and take the dominant plane. Mark all points close to that plane (within a small height tolerance) as ground and remove them. Alternatively, if using Ouster, you can use ring information: ignore the lowest few rings if they mostly see ground except when very close (but note this might miss far-away low obstacles).
   2. **Downsampling:** Apply a voxel grid filter (PCL’s `VoxelGrid`) with a small leaf size (e.g. 0.05 m). This will speed up clustering dramatically with minimal impact on cones. Test this parameter – ensure a single cone still yields multiple voxels (so it doesn’t disappear). A leaf size around 5 cm usually keeps the general shape of small objects but merges redundant points.
   3. **Clustering:** Use Euclidean clustering from PCL (`EuclideanClusterExtraction`). Provide the filtered point cloud and the distance tolerance (cluster radius). For FSAE cones, a tolerance ~0.3 m is a good starting point (since no two cones are placed closer than ~3 m, except maybe in tight chicanes where they could be ~1 m apart ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=In%20the%20end%2C%20we%20settled,would%20aggregate%20in%20close%20proximity)) – still above 0.3 m). Also set a minimum cluster size (maybe 5 points) to ignore tiny noise clusters, and a maximum cluster size (maybe a few thousand points) to catch if something large (like a barrier) is in view – though ideally you won’t have such large objects on track. PCL will return indices for each cluster.
   4. **Feature Extraction:** For each cluster (cone candidate), compute features needed for classification and for SLAM. At minimum, find the 3D position of the cone – you might use the centroid of the cluster or, better, the bottom-most point which likely corresponds to where the cone touches ground (since for planning you care about the base location). You could also fit a small bounding box to the cluster to estimate the cone’s height and width (useful to distinguish cone types if necessary). Extract intensity values of points in the cluster for the classification step.

**4. Cone Classification (Integration in Pipeline):**  
   Now classify each cluster as blue, yellow, orange, or unknown:
   - If using a **CNN classifier**, prepare the cluster’s intensity image. Using the methods mentioned earlier, project the points to a fixed-size grid (32×32). One simple way: take the cluster’s points, transform them into the cluster’s own principal axis frame (so the cone is upright in the image), then assign each point’s intensity to a pixel based on its angle. The Ouster `img_node` example can guide how to map 3D points to image coordinates using LiDAR angular increments ([LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting - MUR Driverless Blog](https://murdriverless.github.io/perception/lidar-sim-clf-mount/#:~:text=time,intensity%2C%20reflectivity%2C%20and%20ambient%20noise)). Pass this 32×32 grayscale image to your CNN (which should be loaded and running on TensorRT). The output might be a softmax with three classes. Decide a confidence threshold – e.g. if the top probability is below 0.6, classify that cone as “unknown” to be safe.
   - If using a simpler **intensity-based algorithm**, compute summary metrics: e.g. sort points by height and take the top 30% and bottom 30% intensity averages. Compare those – a yellow cone might have bottom and top intensities higher than middle. You can craft rules like: “if average intensity of bottom section > average of middle section by X and similarly top > middle, then likely yellow; if the opposite, likely blue.” Include a margin or deadzone where it becomes “unknown” if not clear. Also, **use reflectivity if available** – reflectivity values might directly cluster around different means for blue vs yellow. As a complement, check cluster size or LiDAR return intensity consistency: orange cones (if taller or with reflective tape) might return notably more points or higher intensity outliers (due to reflective band) ([](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/autosec2022_23039_paper.pdf#:~:text=orange%20has%20the%20highest%20average,is%20placed%2020%20feet%20away)). For example, if you detect any returns with intensity above a very high threshold (like >3000 in Ouster units), it could indicate a retroreflective tape – which some orange cones have – thus an orange cone.
   - **Output Format:** For each cone, you’ll ultimately output something like: position (x, y) in the map or vehicle frame, color label, and perhaps an uncertainty. For SLAM, you might convert the positions to the global frame using the current pose estimate (if doing mapping). Initially, keep them in the vehicle frame and let SLAM incorporate them with the vehicle pose.

**5. SLAM and Localization Implementation:**  
   - **EKF-SLAM Implementation:** If writing an EKF-SLAM, define your state vector as `X = [x, y, θ, l1_x, l1_y, l2_x, l2_y, ...]` for all landmarks. In practice, you might not want to pre-allocate landmarks; instead, start with an empty map and add landmarks dynamically. A convenient approach is to maintain a separate structure for the map (e.g. a dictionary of landmark ID -> position and covariance) and only keep the vehicle pose in the EKF state. However, standard EKF-SLAM keeps everything in one vector. You can use an **incremental indexing** when a new cone is observed: extend the state and covariance matrix. Use motion model $[v, \omega]$ from encoders/IMU for prediction (with noise params for drift). The measurement model is $z = [range, bearing]` to a cone (bearing can be w.r.t the car’s orientation). Implement the standard EKF equations (Jacobian of measurement model etc.). Data association in EKF: you’ll have to decide which landmark each measurement corresponds to. You can do a gating by Mahalanobis distance: compute the innovation if it were each known landmark and see which is statistically most likely (this is a form of NN matching taking uncertainty into account). PCL clustering already groups points belonging to one cone in a single measurement, which is helpful. If a measurement doesn’t match any existing landmark (e.g. distance too large for all), initialize a new landmark at the measured position with an initial uncertainty.
   - **Graph-SLAM Implementation:** If using g2o or GTSAM, model the problem with nodes for poses and landmarks. Each LiDAR odometry or IMU integration over a time delta can be a pose-to-pose constraint (though if you have an EKF odometry, you might just use that pose as given and not solve for all intermediate poses to keep graph small). Each cone observation generates a constraint between the current pose node and the respective landmark node (like: pose_i plus observation = landmark_j). These frameworks will require you to supply the Jacobians or rely on built-in sensor models. Graph-SLAM can be updated in batch (after a lap) or incrementally. An **incremental smoothing** approach (like iSAM) could update the solution in near-real-time with each new observation, which might be viable given the small size. Monitor the computation time if doing full nonlinear optimizations frequently. Often, teams run a graph optimization after finishing the first full lap (since that’s when loop closure occurs) to refine the map, then switch to localization.
   - **Sensor Fusion for Localization:** In either EKF or Graph-SLAM, incorporate the IMU and wheel data. For EKF, as mentioned, the motion update uses odometry ([IIT Bombay Racing Driverless: Autonomous Driving Stack for Formula Student AI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.06113v1#:~:text=competition%20tracks,10%20shows%20this%20parallel%20flow)). For Graph-SLAM, add an edge for each odometry increment. If you have a full INS (IMU+GPS in other contexts), here you skip GPS but still use the IMU’s yaw to stabilize heading, since pure wheel odometry can suffer if the car drifts or slips. Many teams also use a **heading initialization routine** (like orienting the car at start so that yaw=0 aligns with track) to simplify.
   - **Map Initialization:** At the start of mapping, you have no landmarks. As the car moves and sees a cone, initialize it. Thereafter, when revisiting the same cone, the SLAM will update its estimate. If possible, differentiate between left and right cones early via color – you can even store separate landmark sets for blue and yellow to reduce confusion.
   - **SLAM Output:** The SLAM module should continuously output the **vehicle pose** (x, y, θ) in some global frame (for example, the frame where the car started is (0,0,0)). This pose will be used by the planning/control modules. It should also output the **map of cones** (at least during debugging/validation). You can publish the landmark positions as a PointCloud or a custom message for visualization in RViz.
   - Test the SLAM in a simulation or off-line with a log to ensure consistency. For EKF, check that covariances don’t collapse incorrectly; for Graph, check that optimization converges. A good sign is that after a full lap, the start and end pose of the car as computed by SLAM are very close (if they are not, loop closure isn’t working well or drift is uncorrected).

**6. System Tuning and Validation:**
   - **Bench testing:** Before running on the car, test the perception code with recorded data. Use a rosbag of a LiDAR scan in a scene with a few cones. Verify that the ground removal is working (no cone points are mistakenly removed) and that each cone produces exactly one cluster. Adjust the ground plane model or height threshold if you see issues (e.g., cone base being cut off means your ground model is too aggressive). Verify clustering tolerance: if two adjacent cones are merged into one cluster, lower the tolerance.
   - **Classification tuning:** Place one blue and one yellow cone in front of the car. Collect intensity data at various ranges. If using threshold method, set those thresholds now. If using CNN, evaluate its output confidence at different distances – you might find beyond 10 m the network outputs “unknown” or random; that’s fine as long as within the critical range it’s accurate. Perform indoor vs. outdoor tests (some LiDAR intensities can change slightly due to ambient noise; ensure your classification still holds). Augment the training data if needed.
   - **Small-track testing:** Set up a mini track (even just a few cones in a curve or line). Drive the car (or manually carry the LiDAR sensor) through this track at low speed. Monitor the SLAM’s map output. A good practice is to measure the actual cone positions with a tape and compare against the SLAM map. Initial errors might be ~0.5 m; try to tune down to <0.2 m by adjusting SLAM process and measurement noise parameters. For example, if the SLAM map is overly jittery and noisy, you may reduce measurement noise assumptions (trust LiDAR more). If it’s very smooth but has a bias, perhaps increase trust in observations or incorporate a loop closure.
   - **Latency measurement:** Even if the pipeline is designed for low latency, measure it. You can timestamp when a LiDAR message is received and when the detection output is published, then compute the difference. Tools like ROS2 tracing or simply printing timing info can do this. Aim for e.g. 30 ms or better. If you find a higher number (say 80 ms), look into which stage is taking too long (profiling printouts in code can help pinpoint). Then optimize that stage (e.g. reduce voxel leaf size further to speed up, or move a computation to GPU).
   - **Integration testing:** Once perception and SLAM seem to work in isolation, integrate with the rest of the stack (motion planning and control). For planning, you will use the cone positions (blue/yellow) to plan a path. Ensure that the perception provides all necessary info (maybe you need to identify the start gate – often two orange cones together – you might include a simple logic to detect that pattern too). Also ensure that when the car drives faster, the perception keeps up. The true test is doing a full **autonomous lap**: start the car, let it build the map and drive. Typically in FSG, the first lap is an exploration lap (slower) – use that to build the map. Then for the second lap (fast lap), you could switch the system to a “localization mode” where you don’t add new landmarks but just localize against the built map (to eliminate any slight map drift during the hot lap). Many teams achieved very reliable performance with this two-phase approach.
   - **Validation metrics:** Keep track of key metrics: cone detection rate (did we detect all cones? false positives?), classification accuracy (how often did we mis-label a cone’s color?), localization error (if you have ground truth or at least a manual map, compare). You can log the SLAM pose and compare to an offline high-precision GPS log (if available in testing) or to known distances (e.g., after a lap the car should roughly end up at the start line). If errors are high, revisit each subsystem – e.g., large localization error might indicate poor data association or a timing issue.
   - **Robustness testing:** Try corner cases like cones very close together (simulate a tight chicane) – does your clustering split them correctly? If not, reduce tolerance or use a smarter clustering for that scenario. Try different weather/lighting (though LiDAR is resilient, heavy rain or dust can introduce lots of noise points; in such cases, you might need to tune filters to ignore outlier points by intensity or return type).

**7. Ongoing Improvements:** After the system is up and running, you can further enhance it:
   - Use the perception output to **auto-generate training data** for better models. For instance, once your pipeline can detect cones, you can record raw point clouds and use the pipeline’s output to label points by cone color (semi-automated ground truth) – this was noted as a useful way to build a labeled dataset for future supervised learning approaches ([MUR Blog - LiDAR Simulation, Classification and Mounting](https://murmotorsports.eng.unimelb.edu.au/news-and-events/news-and-events/mur-blog-lidar-simulation,-classification-and-mounting#:~:text=Another%20good%20outcome%20from%20the,required%20labeled%20point%20cloud%20dataset)).
   - Explore more advanced algorithms when you have time: e.g., a **Kalman filter tracker** for cones could smooth their positions over time (though cones are static, so this mostly helps in filtering out noise). Or incorporate the LiDAR intensity directly into SLAM (some research uses intensity of known colored landmarks to improve data association).
   - Keep an eye on computational load on the Jetson. Use tegrastats (NVIDIA tool) to see CPU/GPU usage. Ensure the system doesn’t overheat or throttle (consider using the high-performance power mode on Orin and adequate cooling, since summer competitions can be hot).
   - Finally, maintain fallback behaviors: if classification is uncertain, have the planning treat the cone appropriately (e.g., unknown cones could be treated as both boundaries until identified). And ensure that even if the SLAM has a momentary dropout, the car can follow the last known good path (perhaps using pure odometry for a short period).

By following this implementation guide and leveraging the cited state-of-the-art methods, you can build a **high-speed, low-latency, and accurate LiDAR-only perception system**. Such a system was key to successes in past Formula Student Driverless competitions, proving that with well-tuned LiDAR perception, a car can reliably navigate and race through a cone-defined track without any cameras or GPS ([(PDF) The Software Stack That Won the Formula Student Driverless Competition](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364515298_The_Software_Stack_That_Won_the_Formula_Student_Driverless_Competition#:~:text=disciplines%20of%20the%20Formula%20Student,diagram%20that%20takes%20the)) ([A Performance Comparison of SLAM-Algorithms for Formula Student Autonomous Driving | springerprofessional.de](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/a-performance-comparison-of-slam-algorithms-for-formula-student-/50428406#:~:text=SLAM%2C%20FastSLAM%20and%20GraphSLAM,datasets)). Good luck with your autonomous racing vehicle development!
